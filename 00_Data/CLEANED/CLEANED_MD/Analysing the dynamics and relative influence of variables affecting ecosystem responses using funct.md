# Abstract (258 of 350 words) 

1. Understanding the relative influence of variables on ecosystem responses and the dynamics of their effect is necessary for effective ecosystem monitoring and management. Also known as causal pathways analysis, we develop an approach using functional Principal Components Analysis (fPCA) and machine learning within a scenario analysis framework.
2. fPCA is used to identify most influential variables for correlated, non-homogenoeus and nonlinear time series data characteristic of complex ecosystems. Hierarchical clustering of fPCA scores reveals groups of more homogeneous scenarios and similarly influential variables. The resultant subset of variables helps to overcome model identifiability problems when analysing time-lagged effects using Boosted Regression Trees (BRT).
3. We use simulated data generated by a Dynamic Bayesian Network (DBN) of ecological windows for seagrass ecosystems given dredging stressors; 3024 scenarios with 75 state variables are analysed. The BRT demonstrated a high level of fit $\left(\left(R^{2} \approx 0.97, M S E \approx 0.16\right)\right.$, supporting the validity of influential variables identified by fPCA. Influential variables identified included genus, location type, light, growth and seed. Six consecutive months of positive growth and adequate light were important for predicting states of high or moderate population.
4. Compared to traditional scenario analysis and sensitivity analysis approaches, our approach simultaneously enabled capture of n-way interactions while accounting for time correlations. Although some variables and their dynamics agreed with existing knowledge, new variables and/or time lags of their effects were identified, corresponding to opportunities for further

investigation as well as informing monitoring and management. Although we demonstrate our method on state variables with DBN simulated data, it is equally applicable to general time series data.

# 1 INTRODUCTION 

Understanding the factors that drive variations in ecosystem response over time and space is critical for anticipative management of complex ecosystems (Walker, Holling et al. 2004). Oftentimes, this includes identification of causal pathways and the relative influence of biotic and abiotic factors affecting animal and plant population dynamics (Gimenez, Anker-Nilssen et al. 2011). Walker, Holling et al. (2004) echo this concept of causal pathways as the analysis of relationships between system covariates, which they refer to as 'slow' variables, and responses, as 'fast' variables. Such analysis of causal pathways has been applied both in the context of ecosystems (e.g. (Gimenez, Anker-Nilssen et al. 2011)) and Socio-Ecological Systems (SES) (e.g. (Richards, Sanó et al. 2013)). Although common statistical techniques such as multiple regression could be used to explore such relationships using regression coefficients and significance, this approach is insufficient as ecosystems are complex systems (Ascough li, Maier et al. 2008, Levin and Lubchenco 2008). Ecosystems often have nonhomogeneous, non-linear responses to biological, environmental and anthropogenic changes where the time delay or lag in the response can be difficult to anticipate, and have complex dependencies between covariates (aka factors). Instead, what is required are analyses suited for heterogeneous, longitudinal data that do not violate assumptions regarding multi-collinearity inherent in many linear models.

Instead of these regression-based approaches, we propose one based on functional Principal Components Analysis (fPCA) and machine learning techniques for analysing the relative influence of variables and the dynamics of their effects on ecosystem responses (Ramsay, Hooker et al. 2009). We

apply these techniques in a scenario analysis framework similar to Pollino, Woodberry et al. (2007) using observations or simulated data generated by a validated, predictive model. Scenario analysis is widely used for assessing management options and understanding ecosystem variability and dynamics (Pollino, Woodberry et al. 2007, Richards, Sanó et al. 2013). A scenario is often defined using a combination of variables set to specific values at specific times. However, the number of scenarios grows exponentially with possible management actions, future disturbances, environmental conditions and biological responses quickly making such analyses intractable except in the simplest systems.

In some ways, comprehensive scenario analysis is akin to sensitivity analysis in that it identifies the most influential variables (Frey and Sumeet 2002). Although automated methods for sensitivity analysis do exist (e.g. (Coupé and Van Der Gaag 2002)), there are challenges in adapting these to the ecosystem context due to time correlated response variables. Additionally, existing methods for sensitivity analysis tend to focus on varying model parameters, whereas scenario analysis involves variation of model covariates in time (see Discussion). Finally, given the heterogeneous nature of these systems, it is often necessary to identify groups or clusters of similar scenarios with more homogeneous data to facilitate modelling and analysis. This is because many modelling methods such as regression assume homoscedasticity. Therefore, the four overarching goals are to identify: 1) the most influential variables, 2) clusters of more homogeneous scenarios, 3) time lags in ecosystem response given covariates, and 4) the most influential variables for specific clusters of scenarios.

Here, we demonstrate the proposed scenario analysis framework on the analysis of ecological windows for seagrass meadows. It uses simulated data generated from a validated, expert-elicited Dynamic Bayesian Network (DBN) model of seagrasses affected by dredging (Wu, Mengersen et al. 2017), which we refer to as the Seagrass DBN (SDBN) for brevity. We are interested here in identifying the most influential variables, similar scenarios, and time lagged effects (goals 1-4 above), affecting seagrass resilience. The case study is illustrated in Section 2. Section 3 describes the proposed

approach, which is demonstrated for seagrasses in Section 4. Section 5 discussesf the proposed approach, its relationship to existing approaches for scenario analysis and sensitivity analysis, and its generalisation for analysis of complex ecosystems.

# 2 BACKGROUND: CASE STUDY DATA 

Our approach is demonstrated here using a SDBN model of seagrass and dredging. Seagrasses are critical primary producers that have been declining globally, in large part due to anthropogenic stressors including dredging (Waycott, Duarte et al. 2009). They provide habitat for endangered species such as the dugong (Dugong dugon) and green turtle (Chelonia mydas), and have been estimated to contribute ecosystem services of $\$ 1.9$ trillion per annum through nutrient uptake and cycling, carbon sequestration and sediment consolidation (Waycott, Duarte et al. 2009). Understanding the dynamics of anthropogenic stressors and their effects on seagrass resilience can help support more effective monitoring and management using ecological windows (Fraser, Short et al. 2017, Wu, Mengersen et al. 2017). Ecological windows are periods during which a specific stressor can occur with minimal impact on an organism or ecosystem, determined from site-specific biological, environmental and stressor interactions (Wu, Mengersen et al. 2017).

The SDBN model contains 28 variables observed or simulated at 73 discrete points in time and factors including population variables (e.g. biomass, shoot density), seagrass resistance (e.g. growth and physiology), recovery (e.g. seed and vegetative fragments), local site conditions (e.g. genera presence, tropical or temperate climate, depth), environmental factors (e.g. light and water quality) and the time of year (Appendix A, Supporting Information). The SDBN codifies interactions and feedback loops between these variables and facilitates bottom-up generative simulation of scenarios, such as scenarios of different dredging durations, light reduction and seagrass phenologies. It is customisable for different seagrass genera and global locations where seagrass meadows grow.

We focus on scenarios of dredging, the timing of dredging, and biological and environmental processes of case study seagrass meadows. One way to mitigate the impact of dredging is the use of ecological windows, periods of time during the year when specific dredging campaigns can meet requirements for resilience based management (Wu, Mengersen et al. 2017). To better understand the influence of variables and their dynamics in the context of ecological windows-based management of seagrass meadows, we investigated 3024 scenarios comprising every combination of the following (for more details, refer to (Wu, Mengersen et al. 2017)):

- 28 global sites inhabited primarily by one of the genera Amphibolis, Zostera and Halophila and which display the three main life history types of seagrasses - persistent, opportunistic and colonising - in tropical and temperate climates (Kilminster, McMahon et al. 2015).
- Dredging designs of duration $0,1,2,3,6,9$ and 12 months, and 6 months dredging with alternating 3 months or 2 months of dredging and rest periods.
- Dredging start times from January through December at monthly intervals

Here, the dredge durations considered were more akin to capital dredging programs given maintenance dredging is typically 2 weeks or less in duration (Ports Australia 2014). Our model assumed constant light stress across the meadow for the duration of dredging.

The data generated by the SDBN from an analysis of the 3024 scenarios above are notated as follows:

- Each state variable $P_{i, j, k}$ encodes the state probability,
- $i=1 \ldots T=73$ indexes a discrete point or slice in time,
- $j=1 \ldots \mathrm{~N}=3024$ indexes the scenario number, and
- $k=1 \ldots K=75$ indexes the state variable.

For example, the population variable shoot density has states of high, moderate, low and zero shoot density, and $P_{i, j, k}$ describes the state probaiblities for each of these four states over time. An

illustration of $P_{i, j, k}$ for one variable in one scenario is provided in Supporting Information Fig. 4.

# 3 Materials and Methods 

Our approach for identifying variable influence and variable dynamics, goals 1 through 4 in Section 1, assumes that the data available encompass a relevant scope of scenarios for a given ecosystem context. The data are assumed to be discrete, or can be discretised, and longitudinal with variables $X_{i, j, k}$ for time slice $i=1 \ldots T$, scenario $j=1 \ldots N$, and variable $k=1 \ldots K$. For our case study context of ecological windows-based management of dredging for seagrass, the state variables $X_{i, j, k}$ are modelled as state probabilities $P_{i, j, k}$ and this notation is used throughout even though the methods could be applied in general (i.e. when $X_{i, j, k}$ are not probabilities).

For goal 1, our task was to find a subset of time-series curves $P_{i, j, k^{\prime}}, k^{\prime} \in \mathbf{k}^{\prime}, \mathbf{k}^{\prime} \subset\{1 \ldots K\},\left|\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}\right|=$ $K^{\prime} \ll K$ that could explain the majority of the variation in $P_{i, j, k}$. Another perspective is that $P_{i, j, k^{\prime}}$ should be a good predictor of the response metric(s), such as shoot density and biomass for our case study. Goal 1 was about identifying the most influential variables $k^{\prime}$ (Section 3.1), goal 2 to cluster scenarios $j$ into more homogeneous groups (Section 3.1), goal 3 to ascertain time lags in response to changes in covariates (Section 3.2), and goal 4 to find influential variables for clusters of scenarios (Section 3.2). Methods for validating the models and findings are discussed in Section 3.4.

The R statistical software environment was used throughout, along with the fda and gbm packages for fPCA and Boosted Regression Trees (BRT) machine learning, respectively (R Core Team). The code and data are archived in supporting information.

### 3.1 Most InfluENTIAL Variables and Scenario Clusters (Goals 1 and 2)

Existing methods in sensitivity and scenario analysis, such as regression or ANOVA, can be used to

analyse the relative influence of variables. However, these methods are ill suited to modelling correlated, non-linear time-series data characteristic of complex ecosystems (Frey and Sumeet 2002). As an alternative, fPCA accommodates this type of data in analysing heterogeneous scenarios, summarising into Principal Components (PCs) the primary sources of variation in the data. fPCA is rooted in Functional Data Analysis (FDA), which can be used to analyse large, complex time series data sets with correlated, non-linear responses where each time series curve is approximated by a smooth function (Ramsay, Hooker et al. 2009). The fPCA approach caters for this complexity by summarising into principal components the primary sources of variation in the data.

The data $P_{i, j, k}$ are logit transformed to facilitate curve fitting without hard $[0,1]$ boundaries, then approximated with functions of time using a basis expansion such that $p_{j, k}(t)=c_{j, k, 1} \phi_{1}(t)+\cdots+$ $c_{j, k, m} \phi_{M}(t)$ for $m$ basis functions $\phi_{m}(t)$; we used b-splines for $\phi_{m}(t)$. Penalised smoothing was used to fit the curves with an empirically determined value of $\lambda=0.1$ for a trade-off between smoothness and fit (generalised cross-validation value) (Ramsay, Hooker et al. 2009).

Applying fPCA to these smoothed curves, we obtained principal component (PC) scores $c_{j, l, k}$ for every scenario $j$, component $l$ (calculated from fPCA), and variable $k$ :

$$
c_{j, l, k}=\rho_{\xi_{l}}\left(P_{j, k}-\bar{P}\right)=\int \xi_{l}(t)\left[P_{j, k}(t)-\bar{P}(t)\right] d t
$$

where $\xi_{l}$ is the $l^{\text {th }}$ eigenfunction, $\rho$ is a probe score, $\bar{P}=\operatorname{mean}\left(P_{i, \forall j, \forall k}\right)$, and $\bar{P}(t)=\operatorname{mean}\left(P_{\forall i, j, k}\right)$. By the nature of PCA, the PCs with the largest eigenvalues, or scores, account for the majority of the variation in the data. Additionally, the scores were derived from a linear combination (dot product) of loadings (weights) and the original data. Thus, these scores $c_{j, l, k}$ are an indicator of the degree to which a variable explains variation in the data. However, the scores are only comparable if the data are appropriately scaled, normalised in our case.

similarly influential variables $k$ through clustering based on distances between scores. Although many different clustering methods could be applied, we adopt hierarchical clustering as it does not require a priori knowledge of the number of clusters (Anderberg 1973). Specifically, we applied hierarchical clustering using Euclidean distances between scores $c_{j, l, k}$ for each PC $l$. For example, PC $l$ may reveal a large life history effect at the genus level, i.e. a high loading or score $c_{j, l, k}$ for $k=$ genus on component $l$ relative to all other loadings. This approach can therefore be useful for revealing groups of variables with similar impact on a given component. The cluster with variables of greatest influence is denoted $\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}$.

Similarly, hierarchical clustering can reveal clusters of similar, more homogeneous, scenarios in terms of how they explain variation in the data. For example, it may identify groups of scenarios clustered by dredging design and seagrass genera with similar temporal behaviour and ecosystem response as demonstrated through the case study (Section 4).

# 3.2 Time Lagged Effects and Scenario Clusters (Goals 3 and 4) 

A challenge with estimating time series models with multiple time lagged effects is model identifiability, as the data are insufficient in terms of volume and/or diversity to estimate the number of model parameters (Lehmann, Fienberg et al. 1998). For instance, including four time lags increases the number of parameters four-fold from population $(t)=\beta_{1} \operatorname{light}(t)$ to population $(t)=$ $\beta_{1} \operatorname{light}(t)+\beta_{2} \operatorname{light}(t-1)+\beta_{3} \operatorname{light}(t-2)+\beta_{4} \operatorname{light}(t-3)$. By identifying the most influential variables $\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}$, we are better able to estimate the magnitude and significance of time lagged effects. As this simplified model is built on the subset of most influential variables $\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}$, the goodness of fit of the simplified model provides an alternate way to validate whether $\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}$ captures the majority of the variation in the scenarios.

We focused on identifying dynamic relationships between covariates and the response. To this end,

we evaluated a number of common methods for dynamic modelling including: linear regression with time lags, functional linear regression, and machine learning methods including BRTs with time lags, and DBNs. Although other methods, or other variants of the selected methods, could be evaluated, we focused on this subset as they achieved a high goodness of fit (see Section 4).

Regression is one of the most common methods used for sensitivity analysis (Frey and Sumeet 2002). For both conventional and functional linear regression, the overall expression is:

$$
P_{i, j, k^{*}}=\beta_{0}+\sum_{q=0}^{Q} \sum_{p \in \boldsymbol{k}^{*}} \beta_{q k^{\prime}} P_{i-q, j, p}+\epsilon
$$

where $Q$ is the maximum time lag, $\beta$ are coefficients and $\epsilon$ is the error variable (typically assumed to be normal). Here, a time lag of $q$ of a variable means that the value of the variable at $t-q$ is used to predict the response at time $t$. The difference with functional linear regression is that the coefficients $\beta_{q k^{\prime}}$ are functions rather than scalars (Ramsay, Hooker et al. 2009).

Unlike regression methods, decision trees incrementally partition the variable space of the explanatory variables such that the corresponding response values are more similar in subsequent partitions (Hastie, Tibshirani et al. 2009). Typically, the algorithms split an existing partition into two new partitions (binary splits) at each iteration and terminates when a specified purity (i.e. similarity) criterion is reached. Typically, the mean of the response for a partition is reported for continuous response variables. These methods can capture non-linear relationships and interactions between variables.

BRTs are a variant of the basic decision tree that are capable of significantly greater predictive accuracy with less propensity to overfit (Hastie, Tibshirani et al. 2009). They iteratively fit to the data an ensemble of simple trees using gradient descent where each subsequent tree is fit to the residuals of the previous tree. This effectively places a higher weighting on misclassified cases which, combined

with simple trees of low depth at each iteration, aims to minimise overfitting while maximising predictive accuracy. The overall model is:

$$
P_{i, j, k^{*}}=\mathcal{F}\left(q=0 \ldots Q-1, p \in \boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}, P_{l-q, j, p}\right)+\epsilon
$$

where $\mathcal{F}$ is a function representing the aggregate partitioning effect across all trees. One of the outputs of the BRT is the relative influence of each variable on the response, which can also be used to rank and thus identify the most influential variables.

Equation (4) can also be used to characterise the simplified DBN except that $\mathcal{F}$ can be evaluated using the posterior marginal (equation (1), Appendix A, Supporting Information). We assumed a simple structure for the DBN as visualised in Fig. 1 coupled with Expectation Maximisation (EM) for learning the conditional probabilities for the purposes of this evaluation (Dempster, Laird et al. 1977). Potentially, other structures could also be learned from data (Daly, Shen et al. 2011).

For linear regression, BRTs and DBNs, we evaluated variants of the model with no time lags, with up to $Q=1$ lag (i.e. Markov assumption), and one with up to $Q=12$ lags to capture effects from the previous season in our case study. However, introduction of more lags increases the parametrisation space which can create problems for model identifiability (Lehmann, Fienberg et al. 1998). Due to software limitations, the functional linear method is only evaluated for $Q=0$.

The above methods of regression, functional regression, the BRT and DBN could also be applied to individual clusters of scenarios as determined from aim 2 Section 3.2. This can potentially identify differences in the dynamics and most influential variables between clusters of scenarios. For example, transitory seagrass meadows fluctuate between presence and absence of seagrass whereas enduring meadow populations do not (Kilminster, McMahon et al. 2015). However, model identifiability is a potential problem due to a reduced set of scenarios in each cluster.

We evaluated the goodness of fit and MSE of the linear regression, functional regression, BRT and a DBN to determine the best method for time lags modelling. Goodness of fit $R^{2}$ is:

$$
R^{2}=1-\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N}(\hat{y}(i)-y(i))^{2}}{\sum_{i=1}^{N}(y(i)-\bar{y})^{2}}
$$

where $y(i)$ is response data point $i, \hat{y}$ is model estimated response, $\bar{y}$ is the sample mean and $N$ is the number of data points.

$$
M S E=\frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N}(\hat{y}(i)-y(i))^{2}
$$

To better understand the strengths and limitations of our approach, we also compared the most influential variables $\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}$ identified using functional PCA with those obtained by applying the best regression method (BRTs, Section 4.2) on the full set of variables $\boldsymbol{k}$. However, model identifiability is a potential issue given the size of $\boldsymbol{k}$ when evaluating time lags (Section 3.3).

### 4.1 Most InfluENTIAL Variables and Scenario Clusters (Goals 1 and 2)

fPCA revealed that the first three principal components (PCs) explained $57.7 \%$ of the total variation and each ensuing component explained less than $6 \%$ of total unexplained variation (Appendix B: Table 1, Supporting Information). For each component, scores $c_{j, k, l}$ which explained the linear contribution of scenario $j$ and variable $k$ to that component were computed. Clusters of scenarios and clusters of

variables for each PC were created using these scores (Appendix B: Fig. 1, Supporting Information). The clusters obtained using PC1 through 3 were virtually identical to those obtained using PC1 through 7 (which explained $77 \%$ of total variation).

The PC scores (Appendix B, Supporting Information) indicated that only a small number of variables had scores significantly greater than zero. This suggested that it was feasible to represent the variation in the data using a small subset of the most influential variables $\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}$. Furthermore, these variables $\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}$ demonstrated grouping structures such that the score for a given variable was often virtually constant across a cluster of scenarios. Given the size of $K$, a binomial test was used to determine whether the median cluster score for a given variable and given PC exceeded a specified threshold with 95\% confidence. An empirically chosen threshold score of 20 (Fig. 2) revealed that of the seven most influential variables, three related to genus (and hence life history), and four to location type, which encapsulates combinations of climate (tropical or temperate), habitat type (deep or shallow, subtidal or intertidal), and meadow form (persistent or transitory).

Lowering the score threshold to 7 revealed additional variables (Appendix B: Fig. 2, Supporting Information) although the scores for genus and location type tended to be significantly higher than those for environmental conditions (above or below light saturation), high or absent seed density, population (zero net change in population, zero population) and zero lateral growth. A total of fifteen variables for $\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}$ were derived from these figures, down from 75 in the original set $\boldsymbol{k}$. The selected cutoffs and thresholds were indirectly validated by the high degree of fit of the simplified models built using $\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}$ (Section 4.2).

In addition to clusters of variables, fPCA also revealed clusters of scenarios. Based on the chosen cutoff for hierarchical clustering, the clusters were grouped by: (i) genus, which express life history, (ii)

location type, as characterised by transitory versus persistent, temperate versus tropical and depth of meadow, and (iii) dredge disturbance (Appendix C: Table 1). Clusters 1, 2, 3, 6 and 10 comprised Halophila meadows with 1-3 being transitory tropical meadows, and 6 and 10 being enduring meadows with generally greater light stress tolerance. Clusters 4, 5, and 7 were Amphibolis scenarios where the latter two were dominated by long dredging duration scenarios. Zostera scenarios were distributed across clusters 8, 9 and 11 through 15. Scenarios in cluster 8 were virtually exclusively for the species Zostera muelleri whilst the others were Zostera marina. Similarly, cluster 13 was dominated by meadows in Greenland. Cluster 9 featured scenarios with generally greater light stress tolerance and the remaining clusters tended to reflect specific sites and timings.

Combined with the high PC scores for genus and location type variables (Fig. 2), this result supports the view that life history, as expressed by genus, and meadow form are integral drivers of seagrass dynamics (Kilminster, McMahon et al. 2015). Therefore, understanding the most influential variables after controlling for genus and location can reveal further insight to the management of specific combinations of meadows and dredging disturbances.

# 4.2 Time Lags and Scenario Clusters (Goal 3 and 4) 

To model time lags in seagrass ecosystems, we evaluated regression, functional regression, BRTs and DBNs (Section 3.2) using the subset of the most influential variables $\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}$ as reported in Section 4.1. We further reduce $\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}$ from 15 to 12 variables by removing below saturation light and persistent-tropical-deep-subtidal nodes due to collinearity, and population zero as it was a response variable. Below saturation light was collinear with above saturation light because light only had two states, and persistent-tropical-deep-subtidal was highly correlated with persistent-tropical-intertidal because nodes that are dependent on these location types have the same expert elicited conditional probability values. Section 4.2.1 discusses the goodness of fit and MSE of the trialled methods, followed by an analysis of time lags for all scenarios (Section 4.2.2), and for individual clusters of scenarios (Section 4.2.3).

Using the relative influence of variables at different lags reported by the BRT, we found zero lateral growth, zero net change in population, above saturation light, and high/absent seed density had significant time lagged effects (Fig. 3). That is, the value of the variable at time $t-q, q>0$ was influential on seagrass population at time $t$. Additionally, as DBN state variables are categorical and mutually exclusive, a strong influence due to zero lateral growth equivalently supports strong influence for non-zero lateral growth states.

Generally, more recent lateral growth had greater influence on low to high population than earlier growth up to 12 months previous (yellow versus green influence for greater lags up to 12 months previous, Fig. 3). As expected, location type and genus remained constant over time and had zero influence at lagged time points. Lateral growth demonstrated consistently high influence (green to yellow scores, Fig. 3). This supports the importance of vegetative growth in maintaining or recovering seagrass populations when the population is not driven to zero (Fraser, Short et al. 2017). However, for the zero population response, the result suggests a correlation between zero population and zero growth in the same month or approximately a year previous, respectively. This suggests seasonal effects on population as exemplified by transitory meadows with times of zero population and growth (Kilminster, McMahon et al. 2015).

Similarly, above saturation light was influential for predicting high and low population where lagged effects were influential from up to seven months prior (green, Fig. 3). This result suggests that having successive months of good light conditions is important for achieving high population, and for growth and recovery from zero. In contrast, seed density had little influence overall except on the low state where high or absent seed density up to six months prior had significant influence (green, Fig. 3). As the dredging scenarios included longer dredging durations that can drive a population to zero, this result supports the importance of seed for recovery from zero population.

The significant influence of lagged effects of variables $\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}$ suggests the existence of historical

dependency and cumulative impacts (Levin and Lubchenco 2008). They suggest a need for monitoring over multiple consecutive months to accurately predict future population levels.

# 4.2.3 SCENARIO CLUSTERS 

Due to heterogeneity in the data, it can be useful to investigate time-lagged effects for individual clusters. Using the scenario clusters identified by fPCA (Section 4.1), we fitted BRTs using: (i) the complete set of variables $\boldsymbol{k}$ with $Q=1$ lags (cluster model 1), and (ii) the subset of variables $\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}$ and $Q=12$ lags (cluster model 2). Both approaches achieved a $R^{2}$ on the order of 0.99 and MSE on the order of 0.01 (Appendix C: Table 2 and Table 3, respectively). Effects of larger lags could not be evaluated for cluster model 1 due to model identifiability and we focused on the top five most influential variables that were not directly related to the response (Appendix C: Table 4).

Comparing cluster model 1 (Appendix C: Table 4) with the most influential variables for all scenarios (Section 4.1) revealed that, although there was general agreement (e.g. growth), additional variables emerged for specific genus-location categories. For instance, time of year emerged as influential for Halophila clusters 1 and 3 for predicting high population, reflective of the transitory nature of these meadows. Ability to resist featured in Amphibolis and Zostera clusters but did not for Halophila where recovery factors were more influential, consistent with current understanding of the life history characeristics of these meadows (Kilminster, McMahon et al. 2015).

Similarly, comparing cluster model 2 (Appendix C: Fig. 1) to the time lag results for all scenarios (Section 4.2.2) also showed general agreement with genus-location specific differences. For instance, lagged effects of light were only more prominent for cluster 3 and to a lesser extent 1 and 10. The absence of lagged effects could suggest an opportunity for reduced monitoring. In contrast, lagged effects for growth, seed and light were stronger for Amphibolis and Zostera, suggesting greater historical dependency (Appendix C Fig. 2 and 3, respectively). Therefore, a benefit of cluster scenario analysis is the ability to customise management and monitoring for specific groups or types of meadows, as the most influential variables can differ compared to non-clustered analysis.

We demonstrated a novel statistical approach to help understand causal pathways in complex ecosystems. Central to our approach here is the fPCA. In complex systems with large numbers of variables, and especially when the number of scenarios is not significantly greater than the number of variables, model identifiability is a significant challenge (Lehmann, Fienberg et al. 1998). Fundamentally, model identifiability can only be addressed by either increasing the number of data points (scenarios), or reducing the dimensionality of the problem (variables). The fPCA provides one way to reduce dimensionality for correlated, non-linear and non-stationary longitudinal data. Scores for each scenario, each variable and each PC were used for establishing the most influential variables. Given the potential for high dimensionality (large number of variables) and diversity (large number of scenarios) in complex ecosystems, hierarchical clustering provided a way to interpret results in a statistically rigorous manner. Scenarios were grouped into more homogeneous clusters and variables into clusters of similar influence.

Identification of most influential variables (goal 1) is not only valuable from a scientific and management perspective, but it also helps overcome the challenge of model identifiability for evaluating time lags in ecosystem response given covariates (goal 3). Of the linear regression, functional linear regression, BRT and DBN approaches evaluated for modelling time-lagged effects, the BRT provided the best fit in terms of $R^{2}$ and MSE $\left(R^{2} \approx 0.97, M S E \approx 0.16\right.$, Section 4.2). Such a high degree of fit independently verifies the validity of the most influential variables chosen by fPCA. Furthermore, the method independently identified many influential variables and the time lags of their impact that were consistent with expert knowledge and/or existing literature, further supporting the validity of the results.

An additional benefit of fPCA and hierarchical clustering is the identification of clusters of more homogeneous scenarios in terms of fPCA score (goal 2). In practice, the clusters not only reflected existing knowledge about grouping by genus and location (e.g. tropical versus temperate), but also

provided insight into meadows and scenarios with similar background environmental conditions and responses to dredging stress for our case study. Further investigation of time lagged effects for individual clusters (goal 4) revealed differences in management and monitoring requirements for meadows within these clusters. The response for some clusters had strong historical dependency on light whilst others showed strong seasonal patterns where the response this season was affected by conditions last season. The findings support the heterogeneity of the data and a need for stratification for which clustering is one approach (Gimenez, Anker-Nilssen et al. 2011).

For our seagrass case study, the results suggest that for moderate and high population states, resistance and growth are more influential than seed on population which agrees with field studies (Rasheed 1999, Rasheed 2004). In terms of time-lagged effects, 6 months of consecutive growth and above saturation light were strong predictors of high or moderate population states. Seed density on the other hand was important for predicting the low population state. These results support and inform resilience based monitoring and management of ecosystems, necessitating the use of both historical and current data to predict ecosystem response given cumulative effects (Levin and Lubchenco 2008, Vercelloni, Caley et al. 2017).

Our study assumed that there was constant light stress during the period of dredging. This assumption may not hold for different dredge mechanics, hydrodynamic properties of the site and spatial considerations such as the movement of the dredge. However, analyses similar to those presented here could be done using more complex scenarios involving these additional factors and temporal variations in light stress. Our purpose here was not to provide comprehensive analysis of the responses of seagrass beds but rather to demonstrate a method for scenario analysis for biological community that is expected to respond in complex ways to many biotic and abiotic factors while interacting with a disturbance agent, in this case dredging.

Such an ability to analyse causal pathways using data or simulated data from a generative model provides a powerful tool when direct manipulative experiments cannot be undertaken to understand

a complex ecosystem, which is often the case (Gimenez, Anker-Nilssen et al. 2011). Our approach, based on scenario analysis using fPCA and BRTs, addresses a gap in the current literature. Existing approaches for evaluating relative influence of variables and time-lagged effects include literature on scenario and sensitivity analysis. Scenario analysis of BNs and DBNs for decision support and model validation typically employ manual evaluation of individual scenarios (e.g. (Marcot, Steventon et al. 2006)(Johnson, Mengersen et al. (2010)(Neil, Häger et al. (2009), (Richards, Sanó et al. 2013)), which is infeasible for analysis of large and diverse datasets typical of complex ecosystems. Automated sensitivity analysis (Frey and Sumeet 2002) provides an alternate approach for evaluating relative influence of variables. Simple sensitivity analysis methods include varying only one variable at a time to observe its effect on the response (Uusitalo 2007). More sophisticated approaches for BNs can capture changes to combinations of variables with n-way interactions (reviewed by Coupé and Van Der Gaag (2002)); Richards, Sanó et al. (2013) describe the use of sensitivity analysis of BNs for analysing causal pathways in SES. However, existing automated methods currently do not allow for nway interactions with correlated response variables as is the case for a time series response (van der Gaag, Renooij et al. 2007).

Convergent Cross-Mapping (CCM) presents an alternate, non-parametric approach to identifying causal pathways in time-series data. However, CCM can be highly sensitive to periodic fluctuations as is the case for many ecological systems, and is also sensitive to noise and changes to the deterministic attractor, e.g. changes to the equilibrium point or limit cycle (Cobey and Baskerville 2016). This is challenging for causal pathway analysis in ecosystems that exhibit periodic behaviour with limit cycles and disturbances can lead to changes to the attractor (Holling 1973). The latter results in nonstationarity, which violates inherent assumptions of stationarity in causal analysis of time series graphs using information transfer (Runge, Petoukhov et al. 2015). However, if the system were stationary, information transfer provides a robust approach for analysing conditional dependencies with respect to the modelled variables and their relationships. Note that there are other graph-based approaches such as Structural Equation Modelling (SEM) which have also been extended to time series

analysis. However, SEM is generally formulated for analysis of correlational relationships and longitudinal extensions often still require stationarity (du Toit and Browne 2007). The features of the methods in the literature are summarised in Table 2.

477 Our approach overcomes the challenges for correlated, non-linear, non-stationary data with possible n-way interactions using a fPCA and machine learning scenario analysis framework. Although the case study used a generative model and state variables, the approach can be applied to the analysis of time series in general. However, our approach can only assess causal pathways given a specific model - it should not be used to assess unmodelled pathways. Potential future work includes extension of the methodology to arbitrary time lags, as the maximum lag that can be evaluated is currently constrained by model identifiability, which is related to the number of model parameters and the size and diversity of the data. Additionally, further research is needed on how best to design monitoring and experiments to support these sorts of analyses. For example, scenarios and variables could be used as design points which could then be selected to maximise some utility, e.g. estimation of cluster means to a certain level of precision, maximisation of distance between clusters, and/or minimisation of MSE

for a simplified time-lagged model. Finally, scenario analysis of time-lagged effects could be extended to other dimensions, such as multi-scale spatial effects.
