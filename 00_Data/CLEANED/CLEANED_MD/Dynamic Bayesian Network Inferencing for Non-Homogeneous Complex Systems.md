# Dynamic Bayesian Network Inferencing for Non-homogeneous Complex Systems

Summary. Dynamic Bayesian Networks (DBNs) provide a versatile method for predictive, whole-of-systems modelling to support decision makers in managing natural systems subject to anthropogenic disturbances. However, DBNs typically assume a homogeneous Markov chain which we show can limit the dynamics that can be modelled especially for complex ecosystems that are susceptible to regime change (i.e. change in state transition probabilities). Such regime change can occur as a result of exogeneous inputs and/or due to past system states; the latter is known as path dependency. We develop a method for non-homogeneous DBN inference to capture the dynamics of potentially path-dependent ecosystems. The method enables dynamic updates of DBN parameters at each time slice in computing posterior marginal probabilities given evidence for forwards inference. An approximate algorithm for forwards-backwards inference is also provided noting that convergence is not guaranteed in a path-dependent system. We demonstrate the methods on a seagrass dredging case study and show that the incorporation of path dependency enables conditional absorption into and release from the zero state in line with ecological observations. The model helps managers to develop practical ways to manage the marked impacts of dredging on high value seagrass ecosystems.

Keywords: dynamic bayesian network; non-homogeneous markov chain; ecosystem modelling; seagrass; complex system.

# 1. Introduction 

Predictive, whole-of-systems modelling plays a critical role in supporting decision makers in the management of ecosystems subjected to anthropogenic disturbances. These disturbances often produce a system response that emerges from interactions between environmental and biological processes in a manner that is non-linear, synergistic or sequential with historical dependency (Holling, 2001; Rauscher, 1999). The emergent patterns are often the result of cumulative effects driven by feedback loops, which are instrumental in controlling complex biological systems and in turn ecosystem resilience (Grzegorczyk and Husmeier, 2011; Walker et al., 2004). Resilience is the ability of a system to "absorb changes of state variables, driving variables and parameters and still persist", (Holling, 1973). Thus, to synthesise key insights from an otherwise overwhelming variety and quantity of information with uncertainty, a whole-of-systems, risk-based model is required. Understanding cumulative effects is especially important to support adaptive management as system collapse can occur due to an accumulation of events (Hughes et al., 2005).

One of the key challenges in understanding and managing cumulative effects is that the local rules of interaction can change as the system evolves leading to path dependency (Levin and Lubchenco, 2008); for example, changes to coral landscapes transform the habitat and thus the make-up of local fish populations and communities (Halford and Caley, 2009). Thus, in a state space model, path dependency refers to changes in the system's state transition rules, not changes in system state (Murphy, 2002) where changes arise dependent on past system states. Note that a change in state transition rules is one approach for capturing regime change, which can occur due to path dependency separate from, or in conjunction with, exogeneous inputs. However, ecosystems are often characterised by epistemic uncertainty, including uncertainty about the state of the system and its processes (Regan et al., 2002; Pollino et al., 2007). Therefore, modelling complex systems under uncertainty with regime changes requires a non-homogeneous probabilistic state space approach (Grzegorczyk et al., 2008).

We focus on the global problem of managing the impacts of dredging on seagrass ecosystems as a canonical example of ecosystem management. Seagrasses provide habitat for many endangered species such as the dugong (Dugong dugon) and green turtle (Chelonia mydas), contribute some $\$ 1.9$ trillion per annum through nutrient uptake and cycling, carbon sequestration, and sediment consolidation (Waycott et al., 2009). However, seagrass meadows have been declining at a rate of $110 \mathrm{~km}^{2}$ per year since 1980. One of the biggest threats to seagrass meadows is dredging in or near seagrass habitats (Waycott et al., 2009; Grech et al., 2011). Dredging occurs worldwide to deepen and maintain navigation channels and harbour entrances (Erftemeijer et al., 2006). More effective management of dredging requires an understanding of the complex mechanisms by which seagrass are affected by it.

The environmental impacts on seagrasses from dredging results from both the

dredging process and disposal of dredge spoils (Erftemeijer et al., 2006). In relation to seagrasses, dredging can impact many environmental parameters and affect seagrasses in myriad ways. Dredging can reduce the amount and quality of light reaching seagrasses, increase sedimentation in the water column, and increase deposition of sediment on the seagrass itself resulting in burial (McMahon et al., 2011; Erftemeijer et al., 2006). Such stresses, which can vary over space and time, can alter the state of seagrass ecosystems in a non-linear manner, and lead to seagrass mortality. In some cases, in the face of such disturbances, seagrass ecosystems can persist and even recover over timescales ranging from months to years, whilst in others, the cumulative effects can cause irreversible change (McMahon et al., 2011). For example, large scale seagrass losses in 1965 and 1974 at Laguna Madre, Texas were attributed to maintenance dredging: periodic, small-scale dredging used to maintain a navigation channel (Onuf, 1994).

# 1.1. DBN Approach 

Bayesian Networks (BNs) present one approach for modelling complex systems that has been applied in a wide variety of environmental modelling and decision support applications (Pollino et al., 2007) as well as in expert systems (Lauritzen and Spiegelhalter, 1988). Also called Bayesian Belief Networks, BNs provide a direct way to address uncertainty and missing data, combine expert knowledge with data, and show good predictive accuracy even with small sample sizes (Uusitalo, 2007). Especially in ecological and environmental domains, expert knowledge is often used to build and even quantify BNs (Caley et al., 2014; Uusitalo, 2007).

BNs provide a framework for knowledge representation and reasoning where variables or factors are represented as nodes $X_{1}, X_{2}, \ldots, X_{n} \in \boldsymbol{X}$ in a directed acyclic graph $G(\boldsymbol{X}, \boldsymbol{A})$ with edges or arcs $\boldsymbol{A}$ (Pearl, 1988). A directed edge denotes causal influence or dependence and describes a parent-child relationship. Assuming a discrete state space for all nodes $\boldsymbol{X}$, the Conditional Probability Table (CPT) for a node is $P(X=x \mid \operatorname{Par}(X))$ where $\operatorname{Par}(X)$ refers to the parents of $X$ and $X=x$ refers to the event that variable $X$ is in state $x$. Note that $G(\boldsymbol{X}, \boldsymbol{A})$ encodes conditional independence assumptions as $X$ is only conditioned on nodes $\operatorname{Par}(X)$ that have an arc connecting it to $X$. The overall joint distribution is:

$$
P\left(X_{1}, X_{2}, \ldots, X_{n}\right)=\prod_{i=1}^{n} P\left(X_{i} \mid \operatorname{Par}\left(X_{i}\right)\right)
$$

where $\operatorname{Par}\left(X_{i}\right)$ denotes all the parent nodes of $X_{i}$. The outputs of the BN are posterior marginal probabilities of the form $P(X \mid \boldsymbol{E})$ where $\boldsymbol{E}$ is observed evidence that serve as the input of the model. However, BNs are insufficient for modelling population or ecosystem dynamics (e.g. cumulative effects) as they represent the system as a snapshot or average over time and can not explicitly capture feedback loops as by definition the network must be acyclic (Uusitalo, 2007).

In contrast, a Dynamic Bayesian Network (DBN) can overcome this shortcoming by replicating the BN model at discrete slices in time and connecting nodes located in different time slices with 'temporal arcs' (Murphy, 2002; Kjaerulff, 1992). Let

# 1.2. Non-Homogeneous DBNs 

Even though a DBN is capable of capturing cumulative effects through time slicing, it is still not possible to model systems with regime change as described in Section

1. Such a change could be instigated by exogeneous factors such as the passing of time as described by Fu and Devaraj (2008). Regime change can also be brought about by path dependency such as disease states at previous time slices as described by Craig et al. (1999). In addition, it can be shown that a homogeneous DBN is not capable of replicating any state probability trajectory $P(X(t) \mid \boldsymbol{E})$; under steady state conditions, we show in Section A of the Supplementary Materials that the slope of this trajectory is always decreasing. Therefore, in order to capture the dynamics of complex systems and especially those with regime change and/or path dependency, a non-homogeneous DBN is needed.

We focus on performing inference using CPTs and not network structure learning. In a non-homogeneous DBN, the CPT $P(X(t) \mid \operatorname{Par}(X(t)))$, which captures state transition probabilities, and even the structure $G_{t}\left(\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{t}}, \boldsymbol{A}_{\boldsymbol{t}}\right)$ can change from one time slice to another (Murphy, 2002). However, changes in model structure can lead to 'over-flexibility' especially when learning the model from data (Grzegorczyk and Husmeier, 2009). If there are short time series segments with multiple models, this can lead to inflated uncertainty in inferences. Additionally, there is the conceptual challenge of whether a change should be reflected in a model's structure or its parameterisation. Typically, it is advisable to maintain the same structure unless there is a fundamental change in system processes (Grzegorczyk and Husmeier, 2009). For ecological systems over relatively short time periods this is unlikely to occur, and where it does, may reflect poor model structure.

A non-homogeneous DBN enables the implementation of 'conditionally absorbing' chains. An absorbing chain is a series of states such that, once the system enters that chain, it can never leave again (Levin et al., 2009); absorbing chains can arise due to feedback loops. Conditionally absorbing chains are useful for capturing regime change as under given regime(s), the system should absorb into a terminal state as an outcome of the DBN (or Markov process) but under a different regime, the system should transition to other states and escape from this terminal state. Consider for example an ecological population model; mortality of individuals can occur due to age and other anthropogenic disturbances. However, dead individuals cannot come back to life (they stay in the zero state) so if a population is insufficiently large following disturbance, the population size goes to zero. The zero state is an absorbing state and may be part of an absorbing chain. If, however, sufficient individuals remain after the disturbance ceases, or individuals recruit from other populations, the population can recover. In order to accommodate these dynamics adequately, the model must be able to transition away from the zero state. Consequently, there are two regimes corresponding to two different sets of model parameters with one that absorbs into zero and one that transitions away from zero. This cannot be achieved with a homogeneous DBN.

This paper proposes a method for structuring and performing inference for nonhomogeneous DBNs with path dependency in spatio-temporal complex systems. We focus on inference and not network learning; learning methods are reviewed by Grzegorczyk et al. (2008) and Dondelinger et al. (2013). Firstly, we review existing approaches to modelling using non-homogeneous DBNs and inference (Section 2). Then we propose an approach for non-homogeneous inference that can adapt

existing inference algorithms such as bucket elimination, clustering and approximate methods (Section 3). Finally, the practical implications of non-homogeneous DBNs are discussed in a seagrass dredging case-study where the implications and applications of conditionally absorbing chains are considered.

# 2. Existing Methods 

To date, non-homogeneous DBNs have been little studied, especially in the field of ecosystem modelling. Grzegorczyk et al. (2008) presented a seminal paper describing a method to learn gene regulatory networks, encoded as non-homogeneous DBNs, from data using Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) with Gaussian mixture network structure allocations. The bulk of the literature on non-homogeneous DBNs is applied in this context of learning gene regulatory networks, e.g. Grzegorczyk and Husmeier (2013) and Dondelinger et al. (2013). However, none of these works specifically investigate the issue of inference in non-homogeneous DBNs in the context of complex systems with path dependency. If a non-homogeneous DBN is completely specified such that the structure and parameters to use for each time slice are known in advance, such as the case for gene regulatory networks (Grzegorczyk et al., 2008), inference can be performed with conventional inference algorithms (refer to Section 3). However, such an approach is not feasible for predictive modelling when there is path dependency.

Although there are other examples of non-homogeneous Markov state space models where transition probabilities have some degree of path dependency, these studies have focused on specific applications and are not necessarily generalisable to DBNs. Fu and Devaraj (2008) present a non-homogeneous linear Markov Chain model of concrete degradation in bridges where the transition probabilities are estimated using pairs of observations over time combined with expert knowledge. In their application, the selected model is purely based on the time elapsed. Craig et al. (1999) provide an example of a linear Markov Chain model of diabetes disease states where the state transition probabilities are captured using a proportional odds model of disease progression dependent on the duration of the disease and its previous severity. In their model, mortality was incorporated using a hazard model. The parameters for the disease progression and mortality models were obtained using MCMC and an observational dataset. Therefore, given an inference scenario, the Markov Chain transition probabilities could be determined in advance using these two models.

The above review reveals that in non-homogeneous DBNs, inference with path dependency remains an area that is yet to be fully addressed. In order to gain insight into how such inference can be achieved, a review of current BN and DBN inference methods is provided below. A focus is placed on exact inference methods as exact inference can then serve as a reference point and benchmark for approximate methods.

# 2.1. Inference 

In general, it is possible to unroll a DBN by instantiating the model at every time slice in the simulation window of interest and connecting temporal arcs as shown in Fig. 1. Doing so creates an equivalent BN from which any BN inference algorithm can be applied (Murphy, 2002). Note that a temporal arc is one that originates in one time slice $t$ and terminates in another time slice $t+k, k \neq 0$. Guo and Hsu (2002) provide a comprehensive survey of exact and approximate BN inference methods. The basic task of inference is to find the marginal belief, $P(X \mid \boldsymbol{E})$ where $\boldsymbol{E}$ is the evidence observed or specified for the inference scenario being run (Pearl, 1988). This is the Bayesian estimation task. Alternatively, Maximum A Posteriori (MAP) inference can be applied to find the states most likely to be consistent with a specified set of observations (Guo and Hsu, 2002).

There are three major classes of exact inference algorithms along with many variations and refinements on each method within each class (Darwiche, 2003). Conditioning based methods adopt an approach that when a variable or node is observed, the topology of the network is simplified by deleting edges outgoing from that node (Pearl, 1988) and conditioning child nodes on it. Examples include loop cutset conditioning (Pearl, 1988) and recursive conditioning (Darwiche, 2001) which is an any memory algorithm in that it can trade off computation time for memory (i.e. space) tractability.

Variable elimination based methods recursively reduce the probabilistic model of $n$ variables to $n-1$. Examples include bucket elimination (Dechter, 1999) and the use of ant colony optimisation to find the optimal order of elimination (Gmez and Puerta, 2002). Finally, tree clustering based methods transform the network into a jointree that is made up of compound variables, i.e. clusters. This includes the widely used clique-tree (clustering) algorithm (Lauritzen and Spiegelhalter, 1988), the differential approach (Darwiche, 2003), Pearl's polytree (Pearl, 1988) and many others (Guo and Hsu, 2002). Note that many fall under the category of sumproduct algorithms (Kschischang et al., 2001). Note also that there are other, less widely used approaches such as arc reversal (Shachter et al., 1990), and symbolic probabilistic inference (Shachter et al., 1990). However, none of the approaches reviewed by Guo and Hsu (2002) are directly applicable to non-homogeneous DBNs.

In the domain of DBN inference, the two key works so far are by Kjaerulff (1995) and Murphy (2002). Both approaches, which are typically applied to Markovian systems, employ a 'sliding window' with backwards smoothing and forwards forecasting and adopt a clustering based approach to inference. A sliding window helps to reduce computational complexity and enables a potentially infinite simulation window. However, the approach does not explicitly consider non-homogeneous CPTs. There is a notable gap for approaches to address non-homogeneous DBN inference that must be addressed to better model complex ecosystems with regime change and path dependency.

# 3. Non-homogeneous DBN Inference for Complex Systems 

Consider the problem of inference in non-homogeneous DBNs with path dependency. The CPT $P\left(X_{i}(t) \mid \operatorname{Par}\left(X_{i}(t)\right)\right)$ for a given node $X_{i}, X_{i} \in \boldsymbol{X}$, at time slice $t$ is dependent on, or is a function of, $P\left(\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{i}}\left(t-\boldsymbol{k}_{i}\right) \mid \boldsymbol{E}\right)$, the joint posterior marginal probability (i.e. previous state probabilities) for some non-empty subset of nodes $\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{i}} \in \boldsymbol{X}$ located at time slices $t-\boldsymbol{k}_{\boldsymbol{i}}$ where $\boldsymbol{k}_{\boldsymbol{i}} \geq 1$ corresponds to time lags for $\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{i}}$. For example, the CPT $P\left(X_{i}(t) \mid \operatorname{Par}\left(X_{i}(t)\right)\right)$ might be updated as a function of the joint posterior marginal of three nodes $\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{i}}=\{A(t), B(t-1), C(t-2)\}$, corresponding to $\boldsymbol{k}_{\boldsymbol{i}}=\{0,1,2\}$. Note that bold notation indicate vectors. As before, the main inference task is to determine the posterior marginal belief $P(X(t) \mid \boldsymbol{E}) \forall X(t) \in \boldsymbol{X}$ recalling that $\boldsymbol{X}$ is the set of all nodes in the unrolled network.

The evidence $\boldsymbol{E}$ corresponds to observations of the system. There are three main types of evidence (Mrad et al., 2015). Hard evidence corresponds to an observation of some state $x$ such that $P(X=x)=1$ and is supported by all inference algorithms. Probabilistic evidence is used to represent uncertainty in the observation. For node $X$, an observation of the probabilities of the states is captured as $P\left(X=x_{j}\right)=y_{j}, \sum_{j} y_{j}=1$ where $x_{j}$ are node states and $y_{j}$ is the observed state probability. In an ecological context, probabilistic evidence can also be useful for representing the proportion of a population in a certain state. There are many methods for inference using probabilistic evidence. One approach is through notfixed probabilistic evidence of which the most common method is Pearl's 'virtual evidence' (Pearl, 1988). The evidence is 'not fixed' in the sense that, given evidence at node $X_{i}$, the introduction of additional evidence at other nodes $P\left(X_{k \neq i}=x_{j}\right)$ can lead to a change in the marginal beliefs for $P\left(X_{i} \mid \boldsymbol{E}\right)$.

This contrasts with fixed probabilistic evidence (also referred to as soft evidence) approaches, typically based on an Iterative Proportional Fitting Procedure (IPFP), where the marginal remains unchanged despite the introduction of further evidence (Mrad et al., 2015). However, soft evidence based inferencing is not necessarily consistent with MAP inferencing. As existing BN and Hidden Markov Model (HMM) literature for Bayesian and MAP inference also focus on hard and virtual evidence, we treat evidence as hard or virtual in the remainder of this paper.

### 3.1. General Formulation

Consider a non-homogeneous DBN with time-slice templates $G_{t}\left(\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{t}}, \boldsymbol{A}_{\boldsymbol{t}}\right)$ (i.e. network structure) and an equivalent unrolled BN $G(\boldsymbol{X}, \boldsymbol{A})$ (see notation definitions in Section 1.1). In general, the posterior joint probability for a node $X$ given evidence in a BN or an unrolled DBN is:

$$
\begin{aligned}
P(X, \boldsymbol{E}) & =\prod_{X_{i} \in \boldsymbol{X} \backslash X} \Gamma\left(X_{i}\right) \\
\Gamma\left(X_{i}\right) & =\sum_{X_{i}} \prod_{X_{j} \in F_{i} \backslash\left\{F_{1}, \ldots, F_{i-1}\right\}} P\left(X_{j} \mid \operatorname{Par}\left(X_{j}\right)\right) \delta\left(X_{j}\right)
\end{aligned}
$$

where $P\left(X_{i} \mid \operatorname{Par}\left(X_{i}\right)\right)$ is the CPT of node $X_{i}$ given its parents, $F_{i}=\left\{X_{i}\right.$, Child $\left.\left(X_{i}\right)\right\}$ is the family of $X_{i}$, and $\delta\left(X_{i}\right)$ is the observed evidence distribution for node $X_{i}$ (e.g. we observe $P\left(X_{i}=x_{1}\right)=0.3, P\left(X_{i}=x_{2}\right)=0.7$ for $X_{i}$ with two states $x_{1}, x_{2}$ ). Equation (2) is re-written for simplicity from Section 4.1 and (7) in Dechter (1999). Note that $X_{i} \in \boldsymbol{X} \backslash X$ is the set of all nodes $\boldsymbol{X}$ across all time slices minus node $X$; time-slice based inference is developed in the following section. The sum-product algorithm (2) can compute the joint probability more efficiently than multiplying together all conditional probabilities and then marginalising out all nodes other than $X$, which is of exponential computational complexity. It achieves this through dynamic programming by iteratively multiplying by connected parent and child nodes that have not already been multiplied, and marginalising (i.e. summing) out the parent node (3). When done in a near-optimal sequence, the computational complexity at each step is greatly reduced. As $P(\boldsymbol{E})=\sum_{X} P(X, \boldsymbol{E})$ is a constant, the posterior marginal probability $P(X \mid \boldsymbol{E}) \propto P(X, \boldsymbol{E})$ is found by normalising $P(X, \boldsymbol{E})$.

In a path dependent non-homogeneous dynamic system, the model $\mathcal{M}^{t}$ is defined for all nodes $X_{i}(t) \in \boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{t}}$ by the set of CPT values $P\left(X_{i}(t) \mid \operatorname{Par}\left(X_{i}(t)\right)\right)$ and structures $G_{t}\left(\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{t}}, \boldsymbol{A}_{\boldsymbol{t}}\right)$ at time slices $t=1, \ldots, T$. It can be a function of joint marginal probabilities $P\left(\boldsymbol{X}_{i}\left(t-\boldsymbol{k}_{i}\right) \mid \boldsymbol{E}\right)$ at previous time slices, exogeneous factors $\boldsymbol{W}$, evidence $\boldsymbol{E}$ and time itself $t$ :

$$
\mathcal{M}_{\forall i}^{t}\left\{P\left(X_{i}(t) \mid \operatorname{Par}\left(X_{i}(t)\right)\right), G_{t}\left(\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{t}}, \boldsymbol{A}_{\boldsymbol{t}}\right)\right\}=\mathcal{F}_{\forall i}\left\{P\left(\boldsymbol{X}_{i}\left(t-\boldsymbol{k}_{i}\right) \mid \boldsymbol{E}\right), \boldsymbol{E}, \boldsymbol{W}, t\right\}
$$

Note that it is desirable to keep $G_{t}\left(\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{t}}, \boldsymbol{A}_{\boldsymbol{t}}\right)=G\left(\boldsymbol{X}^{*}, \boldsymbol{A}^{*}\right), \forall t$ to avoid the overflexibility problem noted in Section 1.2; we assume this to be the case for the remainder of the paper unless noted otherwise. Similarly, we assume Markov models ( $K=1$ where $K$ is the maximum number of time slices between nodes connected by a temporal arc) though the derivation can be expanded to include arbitrary $K$.

Below is a summary of the main notation used here:

- $\boldsymbol{X}$ denotes the set of all nodes in the unrolled DBN.
- $\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{t}}, \boldsymbol{A}_{\boldsymbol{t}}$ denotes the set of nodes and arcs at time slice $t$ respectively.
- $\mathcal{M}^{t}$ denotes the model as made up of CPTs and structure (nodes and arcs) at time $t$.
- $X_{i}$ denote a node, $X_{i}(t)$ a node at time slice $t$ and $\operatorname{Par}\left(X_{i}\right)$ the parent nodes of $X_{i}$.
- $\boldsymbol{\pi}(t) \subset \boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{t}}$ denotes the set of link nodes that have child nodes in one or more future time slices.
- $\boldsymbol{E}$ denotes the set of evidence encoded as observational probabilities of a node $\delta\left(X_{i}\right)$.
- $P_{t}(\boldsymbol{\pi}(t) \mid \boldsymbol{E}), P_{r}^{f}, P_{r}^{b}, P_{r}^{f b}$ denote the partial result of the marginal joint distribution for the link nodes at time slice $t$, the forwards, backwards and forwardsbackwards partial results respectively.

# 3.2. Exploiting power-law structure of complex systems networks 

Studies of numerous real-world complex systems have found that they tend to possess a structure and/or processes that can be represented using a small world network with power law connectivity (Newman, 2003; Watts and Strogatz, 1998). Such a network is a graph where the in and out-degree connectivity of nodes follows a power law distribution, i.e. many nodes have few parents or children, and a small number have a large number of parents and/or children. Note that the network models described in (Newman, 2003; Watts and Strogatz, 1998) are not the same as the DBNs described here in that their focus is on network structure itself rather than than probabilistic inference outcomes of DBNs. However, a DBN model that has a network structure that follows the power law distribution enables the efficient computation of marginals (similar to clique potentials) for each time slice, or equivalent contiguous subsets of the unrolled DBN; a simulation study of the computational complexity is provided in Section B of the Supplementary Materials.

Assuming a constant graph structure $G\left(\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{t}}, \boldsymbol{A}_{\boldsymbol{t}}\right)=G\left(\boldsymbol{X}^{\star}, \boldsymbol{A}^{\star}\right), \forall t$, denote link nodes $\boldsymbol{\pi}(t) \subset \boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{t}}$ as a subset of nodes that have temporal arcs linking to child nodes located at time slices $t+k, k>0$. A partial result for the marginal joint distribution $P_{t}(\boldsymbol{\pi}(t) \mid \boldsymbol{E})$ at time slice $t$ can be obtained by applying (3) over the subset of nodes $\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{t}}$. For any given target node $X(\tau)$ at time slice $\tau$ for which we want to compute the posterior marginal probability, $P(X(\tau) \mid \boldsymbol{E})$ can be computed by combining the product of the partial results in the forwards $t=1, \ldots, \tau-1$ and backwards $t=T, T-1, \ldots \tau$ directions (similar to the forward backward algorithm for HMMs).

$$
P_{t}(\boldsymbol{\pi}(t) \mid \boldsymbol{E})=\prod_{X_{i} \in \boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{t}} \backslash \boldsymbol{\pi}(t)} \sum_{X_{i}} \prod_{X_{j} \in F_{i} \backslash\left\{F_{1}, \ldots, F_{i-1}\right\}} P\left(X_{j} \mid \operatorname{Par}\left(X_{j}\right)\right) \delta\left(X_{j}\right)
$$

The combined result of forwards and backwards inference from all other time slices in the simulation window is:

$$
\begin{aligned}
P_{\tau}^{f b} & =P_{\tau}^{f} \times P_{\tau}^{b}=\left(\prod_{t=2 . . \tau-1} \sum_{\boldsymbol{\pi}(t-1)} P_{t}(\boldsymbol{\pi}(t) \mid \boldsymbol{E}) q_{1}\right)\left(\prod_{t=T . . \tau+1} \sum_{\boldsymbol{\pi}(t)} P_{t}(\boldsymbol{\pi}(t) \mid \boldsymbol{E}) q_{T}\right) \\
q_{1} & = \begin{cases}P_{1}(\boldsymbol{\pi}(1) \mid \boldsymbol{E}) & t=1 \\
1 & t>1\end{cases} \\
q_{T} & = \begin{cases}\prod_{X_{i} \in \boldsymbol{\pi}(t)} \sum_{X_{i}} \prod_{X_{j} \in F_{i} \backslash\left\{F_{1}, \ldots, F_{i-1}\right\}} P\left(X_{j} \mid \operatorname{Par}\left(X_{j}\right)\right) \delta\left(X_{j}\right) & t=T \\
1 & t<T\end{cases}
\end{aligned}
$$

for $\tau>2$. When $\tau=1, P_{\tau}^{f}=1$ so $P_{\tau}^{f b}=P_{\tau}^{b}$. When $\tau=2, P_{\tau}^{f}=q_{1}, t=1$ and $P_{\tau}^{b}$ is as per (6). The computation is modularised by the use of $\boldsymbol{\pi}(t)$ as link nodes between time slices. These are assumed to be multiplied as part of the family of a parent node but not marginalised in (5). Partial results $P_{t}(\boldsymbol{\pi}(t) \mid \boldsymbol{E})$ are computed for all time slices (potentially in parallel) and then propagated forwards and backwards as per (6) (see Section C of the Supplementary Materials for an example). Note however the case where there exists nodes $\boldsymbol{\psi}(\boldsymbol{t}) \in \boldsymbol{\pi}(t)$ such that for each node

$z \in \boldsymbol{\psi}(\boldsymbol{t})$, all of its parents are in $\boldsymbol{\pi}(t), \operatorname{Par}(z) \subseteq \boldsymbol{\pi}(t)$. This requires an additional multiplication by $P(z|\operatorname{Par}(z)) \delta(z)$ in (6) and (7). Note also that it is possible to find alternate configurations $\boldsymbol{X}_{t}^{\prime}$ to minimise the computational complexity; this is discussed below and in Section D of the Supplementary Materials.

Given $P_{\tau}^{f b}$ and a node $X(\tau) \in \boldsymbol{X}_{\tau}$ the marginal belief is:

$$
P(X(\tau) \mid \boldsymbol{E})=\sum_{\substack{\boldsymbol{\pi}(\tau-1), \\ \boldsymbol{\pi}(\tau) \backslash X(\tau)}} \prod_{\substack{X_{i} \in \\ X_{\tau} \backslash \boldsymbol{\pi}(\tau), X(\tau)}} \sum_{X_{i}} \prod_{X_{j} \in F_{i} \backslash\left\{F_{1}, \ldots, F_{i-1}\right\}} P\left(X_{j} \mid \operatorname{Par}\left(X_{j}\right)\right) \delta\left(X_{j}\right) P_{\tau}^{f b}
$$

Observe that (6) and (7) together are equivalent to (3) in that the computation multiplies by all nodes and marginalises by all non-target nodes over all time slices. The only difference between them is in the order and structure of computation.

We assume that the nodes $\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{t}}$ and arcs $\boldsymbol{A}_{\boldsymbol{t}}$ are identical across all time slices except when $t>T-K$ ( $T$ is the end of the simulation window) as there are child nodes of $\boldsymbol{\pi}(t)$ that are outside the simulation window, $t+K>T$. This means that the partial result computed from (5) is identical across time slices $t<T-K$ except when introducing evidence.

The computation of (5) can be performed using any of the inference algorithms reviewed in Section 2.1. Theoretically, the computation of (5) through (7) does not require a power law network structure. However, the memory space required for computing (6) is tied to the size of $\boldsymbol{\pi}(t)$ as well as the maximal tree width (Darwiche, 2001). If the size of $\boldsymbol{\pi}(t)$ is large, it may be possible, especially with a power law network structure, to reduce its size by creating new 'time-slices' $\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{t}}^{\prime}$ by re-labelling nodes in $\boldsymbol{X}=\bigcup_{t=1 . . T} \boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{t}}$. In order to be able to apply (5), (6) and (7) substituting $\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{t}}^{\prime}$ and $\boldsymbol{\pi}^{\prime}(t)$ for $\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{t}}$ and $\boldsymbol{\pi}(t)$ respectively, these new slices must satisfy: (i) there can be one or more but not all (otherwise $\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{t}}^{\prime}=\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{t}}$ ) nodes $\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{t}} \in \boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{t}}^{\prime}$, (ii) $\left|\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{t}}^{\prime}\right|=\left|\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{t}}\right|$ (same cardinality), and (iii) only nodes $\boldsymbol{\pi}^{\prime}(t)$ have child nodes in a different 'timeslice'. The latter condition enables the computation of partial results for each 'time slice' as required for evaluation of (6). Note that computation of (6) requires an adjustment to the calculation of $q_{1}$ and $q_{T}$ to account for the offset introduced by $\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{t}}^{\prime}$. An example method for finding offset time slice structures $\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{t}}^{\prime}$ to minimise the cardinality of $\boldsymbol{\pi}^{\prime}(t)$ is presented in Section D of the Supplementary Materials.

In the case of a conventional DBN or even a non-homogeneous DBN with no path dependency (i.e. CPT values for every time slice are known in advance), (5) through (7) can be evaluated directly using existing inference algorithms to find $P(X(t) \mid \boldsymbol{E})$. However, inference with path dependency requires further considerations as shown in the next section.

# 3.3. Inference with Path Dependency 

The inference task is concerned with computing the posterior marginal probabilities for every node $X(t) \in \boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{t}}$ for every time slice $t=1, \ldots, T$ given observations on some nodes in the form of hard or virtual evidence. The introduction of path dependent non-homogeneity means that it is not possible, in general, to guarantee convergence. Convergence here refers to the combination of CPTs, structure and

posterior marginal probabilities obtained from (7) at up to $K$ previous time slices such that no more updates of the CPTs and structure are needed at each time slice $t$. The two steps required are the calculation of posterior marginals and the update of the model at time $t$. As the two steps both affect each other, convergence may not be reached in a finite number of iterations. This is illustrated with a simple counter-example - refer to Section E of Supplementary Materials. One way to guarantee convergence to a solution, is to limit the number of times the model (CPT, structure) can be updated.

Recall that as the structure of the network encodes assumptions of conditional independence in the BN, any nodes that are not antecedents or descendents (see Section 3.2) of a node $X(t)$ have no effect on the posterior $P(X(t) \mid \boldsymbol{E})$. Therefore, given that $\boldsymbol{\pi}(t)$ defines nodes with children at future time slices, if a node $z(t+k), k>$ 0 is not a descendent of $\boldsymbol{\pi}(t)$, then any observations of $z(t+k)$ will have no effect on the marginal for any node at time $t$.

Consider inference for scenarios where evidence is specified only for nodes $z$ that are non-descendents of $\boldsymbol{\pi}(t)$. Assume that the model $\mathcal{M}^{1}$ to use for $t=1$ is known and those for $t>1$ are subject to regime change such as path dependency. In this case, convergence is guaranteed using a simple forwards updating algorithm since evidence at future time slices have no effect on posterior marginals at previous time slices, thus allowing for iterative calculation and model updating as per Algorithm 1 .

```
Algorithm 1 Dynamic Forwards Inference

        Update model \(\mathcal{M}^{t+1}\)
        Compute \(P_{t}(\boldsymbol{\pi}(t) \mid \boldsymbol{E})\) using (5)
```

# 4. Case Study 

The proposed method is demonstrated on a complex systems model for predicting the impact of dredging on seagrass, introduced in Section 1. This section discusses the seagrass case study, the challenge of absorbing chains akin to the problem first described in Section 1.2 and proposes one approach for updating CPTs for conditional absorption.

Environmental impact assessments (EIA) play a critical role in the management of ecological assets such as seagrass meadows. The ways seagrass meadows are managed depend on the relevant state or national management framework, but for one example, if a meadow is not able to recover within five years of an anthropogenic activity such as dredging, then it is considered a permanent loss (Environmental Protection Agency, 2009). Models play a crucial role in supporting decision makers by predicting the probability and impact of proposed dredging scenarios in light of cumulative effects and the dynamics of loss and recovery. Loss and recovery is typically measured in terms of management metrics such as shoot density, biomass and the aerial extent of the meadow. Existing seagrass models tend to be focused on specific aspects of the ecosystem such as presence/absence of seagrasses, recovery profiles for different species of seagrass and conceptual models; for a review refer to (Wu et al., 2015). Missing is a model of the system as a whole.

# 4.1. DBN Model 

A DBN model of a seagrass system was developed predominantly using expert elicitation, the literature and some data (Wu et al., 2015). The model captures interactions between ecological elements such as shoot density and biomass, and biological elements such as physiological status, seed density and growth rates, and environmental elements such as light availability and the degree of burial by sediments. This model has over 30 nodes within each time-slice (see Section F of the Supplementary Materials). The model is Markovian with a time slice resolution of one month and represents a single meadow. Potential spatial interactions with other meadows are captured via immigrant seeds and vegetative fragments. Model validation was conducted using a combination of expert panel based validation and experimental data. Additionally, bucket elimination (Dechter, 1999) was used to perform inference in (5) through (7) using a simple backwards elimination strategy from given target nodes. This approach is efficient as it eliminates all child nodes of a parent node first, thus significantly reducing the expansion in memory space required when multiplying. For the purposes of this case study, we focus on population measures such as shoot density or biomass as a management outcome of interest.

Using the expert elicited model, the loss and recovery profile for shoot density obtained for six months of maintenance dredging is shown in Fig. 2. It can be seen that there is a seasonal pattern and under initial circumstances, the majority of the probability mass is in the high and moderate states. The dredging disturbance transfers this mass to the lower states and especially the zero state and after dredging, the probability mass stays in the zero state. As shoot density is influenced by other nodes and itself influences other nodes in a broad feedback loop as shown in Fig. 3, this suggests that there is an absorbing chain that absorbs into the zero state. However, as discussed in Section 1.2, the experts qualitatively describe a scenario where the model absorbs into the zero state under dredging pressure as dead individuals can not come back to life, and transition out of the zero state under conditions suitable for recovery (McMahon et al., 2011). This corresponds to two different regimes with different sets of state transition probabilities; a homogeneous DBN can only implement one of these regimes.

### 4.2. Conditionally Absorbing Chains

Before we consider the more complex scenario involving conditionally absorbing chains in the seagrass model, let us revisit the simple network in Fig. 6 of the Supplementary Materials which had CPTs $P(B \mid A)=P(A \mid B)=\boldsymbol{I}$. This network has two absorbing chains, one of which is across states $\left\{a_{0}, b_{0}\right\}$ as $P\left(a_{1} \mid b_{0}\right)=0$ and $P\left(b_{1} \mid a_{0}\right)=0$; note that nodes $A$ and $B$ are in a feedback loop. If any probability mass moves into the $a_{0}$ or $b_{0}$ states, it can never transition out of this chain of states; which means that if the posterior marginal probability $P\left(A\left(t=t_{0}\right)=a_{0}\right)=p_{0}$ at time $t_{0}$, then for all $t>t_{0}, P\left(A(t)=a_{0}\right)=p_{0}$. This is similarly the case for the other absorbing chain based around states $\left\{a_{1}, b_{1}\right\}$. In order to conditionally absorb into or escape from these states, it is necessary to apply a non-homogeneous DBN,

Consider now the seagrass model and especially the feedback loop involving shoot density as highlighted in Fig. 3 (see Supplementary Materials F for entire network). As per the simple example, an absorbing chain can be identified from CPTs by finding states that have a zero probability of transitioning to other states. In this case study, the probability of having non-zero state shoot density given previously zero shoot density and zero recovery is zero. Additionally, the probability of nonzero state recovery given zero shoot density is also zero when in the absence of other recovery mechanisms (e.g. depleted or destroyed seeds).

One way to conditionally absorb to zero and escape from zero for ordinal states (e.g. zero, low, medium, high) is to shift some portion of the movement of probability mass from non-zero states to higher states 'down' by moving probability mass from the zero state to non-zero states. Let $L$ denote the loss node, $R$ denote the recovery node, $S$ denote the net change shoot density node and $S_{t-1}$ the value of $S$ at a previous time-slice. The CPT of $S$ encodes $P\left(L, R, S_{t-1}, S\right)$ and all nodes have states $h, m, l, z$ (high, moderate, low and zero). Conditional absorption to zero or away from zero is predicated on the existence of net positive recovery in time $t$. One way to implement this is to estimate the shoot density $P(\hat{S})$ in time-slice $t$, and calculate the change in shoot density state probabilities $\delta P(\hat{S})$ between $t-1$ and $t$ as follows:

$$
\delta P(\hat{S})=P(\hat{S})-P\left(S_{t-1}\right)=\left(\begin{array}{c}
\hat{p}_{h}(t)-p_{h}(t-1) \\
\hat{p}_{m}(t)-p_{m}(t-1) \\
\hat{p}_{l}(t)-p_{l}(t-1) \\
\hat{p}_{z}(t)-p_{z}(t-1)
\end{array}\right)=\left(\begin{array}{c}
\delta \hat{p}_{h} \\
\delta \hat{p}_{m} \\
\delta \hat{p}_{l} \\
\delta \hat{p}_{z}
\end{array}\right)
$$

To estimate the total net gain or movement of probability mass to higher states (i.e. away from zero) $p_{\text {gain }}$, we can use a weighted average as follows:

$$
p_{\text {gain }}=3 \delta \hat{p}_{h}+2 \delta \hat{p}_{m}+\delta \hat{p}_{l}
$$

Note that there are three possible state transitions corresponding to movement to a higher state ending in $h(z \rightarrow h, l \rightarrow h, m \rightarrow h)$, two to $m$, and one to $l$ - hence the weightings above. Also, note that state probabilities sum to $1, \sum \delta P(\hat{S})=0$, so any increase in probability for one state has corresponding drops in probability for other state(s).

Given $p_{\text {gain }}$, we can now re-distribute this gain to enable conditional escape from the zero state. One way to do this is to recursively distribute positive $p_{\text {gain }}$ over $z, l, m, h$ states (in that order) using Algorithm 3 by subtracting $\min \left(p_{\text {gain }}, p_{i, t-1}\right)$ (i.e. subtract probability mass to be moved to higher levels) and adding $p_{\text {gain2 }}$ at the next level up (i.e. add what was subtracted).

We use empirical data collected from light shading experiments intended to mimic dredging impacts for an Amphibolis seagrass meadow in Jurien Bay, Australia (McMahon et al., 2011; Lavery et al., 2009). These experiments quantified the impact on shoot density and growth of different shading (light deprivation) intensities (high, moderate, zero shading) in summer and winter for different shading durations ( 3,6 , and 9 months). The study was longitudinal, capturing for each plot of seagrass the change in shoot density and growth rate before shading, immediately after shading was removed, and some time afterwards to observe recovery. A simple Bayesian multinomial model was used to convert observed values into state probabilities (see Section G of the Supplementary Materials). Using this study, the average Mean Squared Error (MSE) was 0.02 across all shoot density states (high, moderate, low and zero) and for just the zero state by itself (see Supplementary Materials Table 2 for individual results categorised by experimental design). The zero state is of particular interest in conservation as it is tied to the risk of loss. For lateral growth the mean MSE was 0.016 and 0.020 for just the zero state. Given that the model and CPTs were expert elicited, these results demonstrate the utliity of the modelling approach in obtaining a good match to the data.

Our results also bring to light a need for further research to extend the pioneering learning algorithms of Grzegorczyk et al. (2008) to path dependent DBNs where both the CPT and the CPT update rules need to be learned. Additionally, this is just one approach to implement conditional absorption and by extension path dependent behaviour. An alternate approach could be to assign constant conditional probabilities to the CPT upon the detection of positive $p_{\text {gain }}$. This contrasts with the proposed method in that the rate of change in state transitions (the value of the CPTs) is independent of the posterior marginals at the previous time slice(s).

This is achieved through exploiting the template nature of DBNs and the power law connectivity of complex systems networks, enabling the decomposition of the problem into modular structures based on time slices and link nodes. The modular structure enables the use of existing exact and approximate inference algorithms to compute posterior marginal probabilities whilst iteratively updating CPTs in the next time slice in the forwards inferencing scenario. In addition, an algorithm was proposed for an approximate solution for forwards and backwards inferencing as it was shown that convergence with dynamic CPT updating (as per a path dependent system) in general cannot be guaranteed. The methodology is demonstrated on the prediction of seagrass meadow trajectories and enables the capture of path dependent regime change such as enabling conditional absorption and release from the zero state under dredging and recovery regimes. Only the predicted trajectories of the non-homogeneous model replicated the loss and recovery profiles of seagrass meadows as described by experts and data. Furthermore, when compared to real-world empirical studies, the model predicted variations in biomass and growth subject to light stress matched observed values closely. This demonstrates the utility of the approach especially as model CPTs and structure were expert elicited. Note that an alternative approach could be to use a single high complexity model that captures all possible regimes; however, this potentially reduces model interpretability, maintainability and computational tractability.

Future work includes the extension of the methods to spatio-temporal or indeed $n$-dimensional models in general with a modular structure. Non-homogeneous

inference across the spatial and temporal domains give rise to the modelling of interactions between populations (such as the case for megafauna), regime shifts and ultimately ecosystem resilience (Holling, 1973; Levin and Lubchenco, 2008). Potentially, there may even be links between criteria for system stability, such as limit points or cycles (Holling, 1973), and convergence could be investigated.

Our seagrass dredging model case study demonstrates the utility and necessity for non-homogeneous inference when dealing with complex ecological systems. The model was developed in collaboration with an interdisciplinary team of scientists comprising biologists, industry representatives, government agencies and statisticians. It has contributed to the outcomes of the Western Australia Marine Science Institution (WAMSI) Dredging Science Node, which seeks to address knowledge gaps in relation to predicting and managing the impact of dredging on marine ecosystems (Short et al., 2016). Given the global prevalence of dredging in marine ecosystems including seagrass ecosystems and the critical role these systems play environmentally and economically, providing a complex systems model addresses a critical need to help develop practical ways to meet the needs of conservation and industry by identifying the best times for dredging to occur through enhancing the resistance and or/recovery of the system to the impact.
