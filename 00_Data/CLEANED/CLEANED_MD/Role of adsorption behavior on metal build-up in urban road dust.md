# Role of adsorption behaviour on metal build-up in urban road dust 

Ayomi Jayarathne ${ }^{1}$, Buddhi Wijesiri ${ }^{1,2, *}$, Prasanna Egodawatta ${ }^{1}$, Godwin A. Ayoko ${ }^{1}$, Ashantha Goonetilleke ${ }^{1}$

\author{

2. College of Chemistry and Environmental Engineering, Shenzhen University, Shenzhen 518060, China


#### Abstract

Metal pollution of stormwater runoff can cause potential toxic impacts on the receiving water environment and human health. Effective pollution mitigation requires accurate stormwater quality modelling. Even though a significant knowledge base exists on the factors influencing metal build-up on urban roads, very limited studies have investigated how metal-particulate interaction influences metal build-up. This study quantitatively assessed the influence of particulate characteristics, together with vehicular traffic and land use, on the build-up of $\mathrm{Zn}, \mathrm{Cu}, \mathrm{Pb}, \mathrm{Cr}, \mathrm{Ni}$ and Cd on urban roads. The study outcomes revealed that the variability in metal build-up is highly influenced by the variability associated with metal adsorption to particulates. The percentage contribution from particulate properties influencing metal adsorption in the case of $<150 \mu \mathrm{~m}$ size road dust particles was found to be higher ( $\mathrm{Zn} 44 \%, \mathrm{Cu} 52 \%, \mathrm{Cr} 16 \%, \mathrm{Ni} 27 \%$ and $\mathrm{Cd} 45 \%$ ) when compared to traffic and land use characteristics ( $\mathrm{Zn} 21 \%, \mathrm{Cu} 13 \%, \mathrm{Cr}$ and $\mathrm{Ni}<10 \%$ and $\mathrm{Cd} 34 \%$ ). Similar adsorption behaviour was noted for metals associated with $>150 \mu \mathrm{~m}$ size road dust particles. Among different particulate properties influencing metal adsorption, effective cation exchange capacity showed a strong positive relationship with the build-up of Cd compared to other metals, highlighting the potential role of Cd in stormwater quality as a readily available metal. The build-up of metals such as Cr and Ni are highly influenced by metal oxides of Al , Fe and Mn and clay forming minerals, indicating that Cr and Ni are relatively stable in nature.

# Introduction 

A significant knowledge base exists on the factors influencing metal build-up on urban roads. However, very limited research studies have investigated metal-particulate interactions during build-up which undermines the accuracy of stormwater quality modelling, and in turn effectiveness of stormwater pollution mitigation strategies (Gunawardana et al., 2015; Jayarathne et al., 2018b; Zhang et al., 2015). This is a challenging issue, because metals builtup on urban roads are washed-off by stormwater runoff and discharged into receiving waters, posing adverse impacts on human and ecosystem health (Daly et al., 2014; Jaishankar et al., 2014; Ma et al., 2017). High concentrations of metals in urban stormwater can cause shortand long-term human health impacts such as skin irritations, breathing problems and carcinogenic effects in lungs and kidneys (Martin and Griswold, 2009; Shi et al., 2011). In aquatic environments, metals can bioaccumulate in the food chain (El-Moselhy et al., 2014; Tao et al., 2012).

Build-up is a fundamental pollutant process, where particle-bound metals accumulate on urban impervious surfaces such as roads over dry weather periods. As illustrated in Fig. 1, build-up is a combination of several processes including deposition, re-suspension, aggregation and re-deposition. A range of natural and anthropogenic factors influence these processes. During these processes, the accumulated metal loads can change, both temporally and spatially. These variations result from: (1) the changes to particulate load accumulated (Patra et al., 2008; Zhao et al., 2017); and (2) the changes to metal affinity to particles (Jayarathne et al., 2018b; Zhang et al., 2015).

Both, particulate accumulation and interactions between particles and metals are considered to collectively influence the metal build-up process. As such, particulate load accumulated on urban surfaces change over the antecedent dry period, during which the particle physicochemical properties (e.g. particle size, organic and metal-oxide coatings, effective cation exchange capacity and specific surface area) are also subject to change (Adachi and Tainosho, 2005; Bian and Zhu, 2009). These physico-chemical properties influence the particle surface charge characteristics (i.e. presence of different organic and inorganic functional groups), which in turn influences the affinity of metals for particles. Thus, metal affinity to particles would change, varying the load and composition of accumulated metals (Gunawardana et al., 2015; Zhang et al., 2016).

Several past research studies have identified the factors that affect changes in particulate load and thereby, the metal build-up load on urban road surfaces, and quantified the degree of influence. Liu et al. (2016) investigated the relationship between metal build-up load and external factors such as traffic volume, land use, distance to arterial roads, road surface roughness and antecedent dry days and found that the variability in metal build-up load decreases with increasing traffic volume. Wicke et al. (2012) studied the role of antecedent dry period and different impervious road surface characteristics on the variability in particulate load and metal build-up. Further, Zhao et al. (2017) quantified the influence of particle size variability on the contamination load of metals in road dust. Therefore, it is also essential to quantify the contribution from the adsorption process and its relative ranking among other influential factors.

Accordingly, this study investigated the variability associated with metal build-up process taking into consideration the adsorption characteristics of particles and external factors, including traffic and land use characteristics. The outcomes of this study will contribute to enhancing stormwater quality modelling outcomes and promote the development of more effective stormwater pollution mitigation strategies.

# 1 Materials and methods 

### 1.1 Study sites and sample collection

Road dust samples were collected from six road sites from two suburbs, Benowa and Nerang in Gold Coast, Australia. All study sites are located in close proximity to Nerang River, which is one of the main waterways within the study area. Fig. 2 shows the selected study sites with percentage fraction of different land uses and their corresponding daily traffic volumes (DTV). The percentage fraction of the different land use types consisting of commercial, residential and industrial were determined considering an area enclosed by a circle of 1 km radius at each study site (Ma et al., 2016). Sampling was carried out for antecedent dry days (ADD) of 1, 4, 7 and 11 days, accounting for different pollutant build-up rates over dry weather periods. For sample collection, a vacuum system was used, the details of which can be found elsewhere (Jayarathne et al., 2017).

### 1.2 Laboratory testing

Road dust samples were initially fractionated into four size fractions, $0.45-75 \mu \mathrm{~m}, 75-150$ $\mu \mathrm{m}, 150-300 \mu \mathrm{~m}$ and $300-425 \mu \mathrm{~m}$ using wet and dry sieving as described by Jayarathne et al. (2017), and analysed for metals and particulate properties. However, Jayarathne et al. (2018a) reported that based on the similarity in build-up characteristics, road dust samples can be grouped into two primary size fractions, $<150 \mu \mathrm{~m}$ (i.e. $0.45-75 \mu \mathrm{~m}$ and $75-150 \mu \mathrm{~m}$ ) and $>150$ $\mu \mathrm{m}$ (i.e. $150-300 \mu \mathrm{~m}$ and $300-425 \mu \mathrm{~m}$ ). Past research on particle behaviour during build-up have also confirmed that size fractions of $<150 \mu \mathrm{~m}$ and $>150 \mu \mathrm{~m}$ in road dust exhibit different build-up patterns. Wijesiri et al. (2015) and Gbeddy et al. (2018) for example, identified that the build-up of $<150 \mu \mathrm{~m}$ particles decreases and the build-up of $>150 \mu \mathrm{~m}$ particles increases over the antecedent dry period. This is because fine particles are more susceptible for redistribution than coarser particles. Moreover, in terms of pollutant load, significant differences in the affinity of metals for $<150 \mu \mathrm{~m}$ and $>150 \mu \mathrm{~m}$ size fractions have been reported (Duong and Lee, 2011; Zhao et al., 2010). Pollutants are intrinsically bound to fine size fraction of particles due to their high specific surface area. As such, the significance of the $<150 \mu \mathrm{~m}$ and $>150 \mu \mathrm{~m}$ size fractions can be highlighted in terms of their behaviour and the amount of associated pollutants during build-up. Accordingly, the data analysis undertaken was grouped into particle size fractions of $<150 \mu \mathrm{~m}$ and $>150 \mu \mathrm{~m}$.

Road dust samples were analysed for nine metals, namely, $\mathrm{Zn}, \mathrm{Cu}, \mathrm{Pb}, \mathrm{Cr}, \mathrm{Ni}, \mathrm{Cd}, \mathrm{Fe}, \mathrm{Mn}$ and Al . These metals are of both, anthropogenic and natural origin and commonly found on urban road surfaces (Adachi and Tainosho, 2005; Duong and Lee, 2011). For example, Zn , $\mathrm{Cu}, \mathrm{Pb}, \mathrm{Cr}, \mathrm{Ni}$ and Cd are primarily released from vehicular traffic related sources and cause potentially toxic impacts on human health and the environment (Christoforidis and Stamatis, 2009; Ma et al., 2016). On the other hand, geogenic sources such as soil erosion contribute to the accumulation of $\mathrm{Fe}, \mathrm{Al}$ and Mn on urban surfaces.

Metal concentrations were determined using the three-step BCR sequential extraction (the Community Bureau of Reference) and aqua regia digestion method as described by Jayarathne et al. (2017). This enabled the separation of the metal concentrations into exchangeable, reducible, oxidisable and residual fractions and the total metal concentration (in $\mathrm{mg} / \mathrm{kg}$ ) considered for data analyses was the summation of the metal concentrations found in sequentially extracted fractions. As part of the quality control/quality assurance (QC/QA) procedures, BCR-701 certified reference material was analysed. The recovery values confirmed the satisfactory efficiency of the method (Appendix A Table S1). The repeatability of the test methods were determined by analysing three replicates of a randomly selected sample within each batch.

Furthermore, total organic carbon (TOC), specific surface area (SSA), effective cation exchange capacity (ECEC) and mineralogical content of road dust were taken as the primary physical and chemical properties that influence metal adsorption to particles (Bradl, 2004; Gunawardana et al., 2014; Rieuwerts et al., 1998). Considering the acidic nature of tropical soils in Queensland State, the effective cation exchange capacity was measured instead of cation exchange capacity (Dawes and Goonetilleke, 2003). The experimental procedures adopted are summarised in Appendix A Table S2.

# 1.3 Bayesian Network (BN) modelling 

Among different modelling approaches, Bayesian Networks (BNs) has become an increasingly popular method for modelling complex environmental systems (Aguilera et al., 2011; Uusitalo, 2007). This method enables the user to integrate both, quantitative and qualitative data whilst the network (model structure) can be upgraded as new knowledge

becomes available (Borsuk et al., 2004). Considering the advantages of BNs, a range of research studies have been carried out to understand different phenomena. For example, Nojavan et al. (2014) studied the anthropogenic and environmental impacts on freshwater and estuarine ecosystems, whilst Ticehurst et al. (2007) assessed the sustainability of coastal lakes. Moreover, Kotta et al. (2009) and Forio et al. (2015) analysed the significance of climate variables on fresh and marine water quality. However, except for a limited number of studies (Li et al., 2017; Wijesiri et al., 2018a, 2018b), this approach has not been widely used to study the inherent processes in relation to urban stormwater pollution.

Bayesian Networks (BNs) develop probabilistic relationships that may exist among a set of variables of a system or a process (Borsuk et al., 2004; McDonald et al., 2015). Such relationships are derived based on the prior knowledge/information or expert opinion. In BNs, relationships among variables are graphically shown using a directed acyclic diagram, where each variable is represented by a node (arcs) and interdependencies between variables are represented by edges (arrows). The strength of the interdependencies can be quantified as conditional regression coefficients (for continuous variables) and/or conditional probabilities (for discrete variables).

In this study, a BN was used to investigate the primary factors believed to have impacts on metal build-up on road surfaces and to understand the process variability. The input data included were: size-fractionated metal concentrations in relation to total build-up load, TOC, ECEC, SSA and the common soil minerals identified by mineralogical analysis (i.e., microcline, albite, chlorite, illite, quartz and amorphous matter), ADDs, DTV and the percentage fraction of land use areas. In this study, metals such as $\mathrm{Fe}, \mathrm{Mn}$ and Al were considered as important soil constituents that facilitate metal adsorption. These metals are commonly found as oxide or hydroxide forms and usually present as either a coating on clay minerals or complex with organic matter (Gunawardana et al., 2015). Accordingly, the variability associated with six metals, namely, $\mathrm{Zn}, \mathrm{Cu}, \mathrm{Pb}, \mathrm{Cr}, \mathrm{Ni}$ and Cd was investigated. For the BN analysis, the bnlearn R statistical computing package and MATLAB R2016b software were used. For each size fraction (i.e. $<150 \mu \mathrm{~m}$ and $>150 \mu \mathrm{~m}$ fraction), separate BN models were developed. The raw data matrix consisted of 48 objects, including 6 sites $\times 4$ ADDs $\times<150 \mu \mathrm{~m}$ size fractions $(0.45-75 \mu \mathrm{~m}$ and $75-150 \mu \mathrm{~m})$ or $>150 \mu \mathrm{~m}$ size fractions

(150-300 $\mu \mathrm{m}$ and $300-425 \mu \mathrm{~m}$ ). In addition to analysing the goodness-of-fit of the measured data and predicted values, Leave-One-Out cross validation was also performed in order to validate the BN modelling outcomes.

# 2 Results and discussion 

### 2.1 Developing relationships between metal build-up and influential factors

The proposed BN (Fig. 3) shows the metal build-up on road surfaces as a collective process of particulate build-up during dry weather periods and metal adsorption to particles. The proposed structure of the BN was assumed to be fixed in relation to the factors analysed in this study. However, the given structure can be improved by changing the factors and their interdependencies as new knowledge is created, thus enhancing the model performance (Wijesiri et al., 2018b).

Table 1 and Table 2 summarises the estimated conditional regression coefficients for the build-up of $\mathrm{Zn}, \mathrm{Cu}, \mathrm{Pb}, \mathrm{Cr}, \mathrm{Ni}$ and Cd in the $<150 \mu \mathrm{~m}$ and $>150 \mu \mathrm{~m}$ size fractions, respectively. The results of the analysis of prediction performance of the BN model for $<150$ $\mu \mathrm{m}$ and $>150 \mu \mathrm{~m}$ size fractions are shown in Fig. 4 and Fig. 5, respectively. As evident, the predicted values of the BN fit well with the observed data. The validity of the BN model is also evident from the cross validation results (Root Mean Square Error - RMSE) given in Appendix A Table S3.

### 2.2 Build-up of metals in $<150 \mu \mathrm{~m}$ size fraction

As evident from Table 1, metal build-up in the $<150 \mu \mathrm{~m}$ particle size fraction can be distinguished, such that: (1) Zn and Cu have positive relationships with traffic volume, but inverse (negative) relationships with land use; (2) $\mathrm{Pb}, \mathrm{Cr}$ and Ni have positive relationships with land use, but inverse relationships with traffic volume; and (3) Cd has positive relationships with both, traffic volume and land use. These observations imply that in terms of pollutant sources, traffic volume plays a more significant role in the build-up of $\mathrm{Zn}, \mathrm{Cu}$ and Cd associated with $<150 \mu \mathrm{~m}$ road dust particulates than land use. These metals are mainly generated through exhaust emissions and wear of tyres and brakes (Mummullage et al., 2014; Świetlik et al., 2015). Further, the influence of traffic volume on the build-up of Zn

is higher than that of Cd and Cu (conditional regression coefficient for $\mathrm{Zn} 1.040, \mathrm{Cu} 0.806$ and $\mathrm{Cd} 0.674$ ). The role of different traffic characteristics on the build-up of $\mathrm{Zn}, \mathrm{Cu}$ and Cd on urban roads has also been reported in previous research studies. According to Liu et al. (2016), the build-up of Zn and Cu is highly variable in the moderate traffic areas, whilst Cd build-up shows significant variability in high traffic areas.

The significance of the relationships identified through BN modelling was further realised through Leave-One-Out cross validation. Cross validation results for the build-up of metals in $<150 \mu \mathrm{~m}$ size fraction are given in the Supplementary Information. Zn has the most significant statistical relationship ( $p<0.01$ ) with DTV compared to other external factors, followed by $\mathrm{Cu}(p<0.01)$ and $\mathrm{Cd}(p<0.05)$. This implies that the variability in the build-up of different metals is influenced by different traffic characteristics. On the other hand, the higher build-up of Zn can be potentially due to the particulate characteristics such as the presence of favourable charge sites on the particle surface and their higher adsorption capacity, that can enhance the interaction of high amounts of Zn .

Moreover, Zn shows positive relationships with the majority of soil constituents (TOC, Fe, albite, microcline, chlorite, illite, quartz and amorphous), which determine the surface charge characteristics of fine particles (Table 1). These factors together contribute $44 \%$ of influence with respect to other soil constituents. Furthermore, in relation to Zn , the contribution from metal adsorption characteristics (i.e. TOC, Fe, albite, microcline, chlorite, illite, quartz and amorphous in Table 1) is significantly higher than the positive influence exerted by traffic volume ( $21 \%$ ). This implies that compared with external influential factors, variability in the build-up of Zn is highly influenced by the adsorption behaviour of particulates.

In the second group of metals, the primary pathway for build-up of $\mathrm{Pb}, \mathrm{Cr}$ and Ni associated with $<150 \mu \mathrm{~m}$ size fraction can be attributed to natural or anthropogenic sources specific to different land uses rather than traffic volume due to the inverse relationships (Table 1). These metals show statistically significant relationships with commercial, residential and industrial land uses ( $p<0.01$ ). Even though the traffic volume does not act as a dominant source for the build-up of $\mathrm{Pb}, \mathrm{Cr}$ and Ni , such metals can be redistributed on road surfaces due to vehicle induced turbulence. Therefore, the inverse relationships between traffic volume and metals

could be due to redistribution by traffic. Among three different land uses, the build-up of Pb , Cr and Ni is more influenced by either commercial or residential land uses rather than industrial land use. This is attributed to the variable nature of land use related activities that can contribute different loads and compositions of metals.

The build-up of Cr and Ni associated with $<150 \mu \mathrm{~m}$ particles increases (positive relationship) with antecedent dry days compared to other metals (Table 1). On the other hand, SSA, metal oxides of Fe and Al and albite in particles $<150 \mu \mathrm{~m}$ exert a significantly large and positive influence on the build-up of Cr and Ni . Based on the cross validation, the significance of both Cr and Ni with Fe is $0.04(p<0.05)$. Fe oxide contributes $16 \%$ and $27 \%$ of influence in relation to Cr and Ni adsorption, respectively, compared to other positive influential factors, including ADD and land use ( $<10 \%$ ). In fact, these minerals enrich the density of surface functional groups, influencing the metal adsorption capacity and thereby the build-up of Cr and Ni over dry weather periods.

As evident from Table 1, the build-up of $\mathrm{Cu}, \mathrm{Pb}$ and Cd is positively influenced by TOC, clay minerals of chlorite and illite and metal oxides of $\mathrm{Fe}, \mathrm{Al}$ and Mn . TOC and Fe oxide exert the highest positive influence on the build-up of Cu (conditional regression coefficients of 0.126 and 2.118 , respectively) followed by Pb (conditional regression coefficients of 0.086 and 1.382 , respectively). Cu is favoured for chemisorption reaction with the surface functional groups present on organic matter and metal oxides (Bradl, 2004; Zhang and Wang, 2009). Furthermore, it has been reported that Cu tends to transform with time into relatively stable geochemical binding forms such as metal oxides and organic matter bound forms from the less stable geochemical forms (Jalali and Khanlari, 2008; Jayarathne et al., 2018b). As observed in this study, TOC and metal oxides together contribute $52 \%$ of influence on Cu adsorption, which is higher than the influence of traffic volume ( $13 \%$ ), implying the significance of adsorption characteristics of Cu on the build-up process. Furthermore, ECEC shows the highest positive influence on Cd adsorption, suggesting that desorption characteristics of Cd would be higher compared to the other metal ions. In fact, it has been reported that Cd tends to persist in exchangeable form in road dust rather than being converted into a stable form over time (Jayarathne et al., 2018b).

# 2.3 Build-up of metals in $>150 \mu \mathrm{~m}$ size fraction 

Similar to metals in the $<150 \mu \mathrm{~m}$ size fraction, in terms of pollutant sources, three groups can be distinguished based on the relationships of metals with traffic volume and land use (Table 2). Zn exhibits a positive relationship with traffic volume, but an inverse relationship with land use, whilst $\mathrm{Cu}, \mathrm{Pb}, \mathrm{Cr}$ and Ni have positive relationships with land use, but inverse relationships with traffic volume. On the other hand, Cd has positive relationships with both, traffic volume and land use. This implies that build-up of Zn and Cd associated with coarser particles is also significantly influenced by traffic volume rather than land use. Zn exhibits a high statistically significant relationship with DTV ( $p<0.05$ ). However, compared with $<150$ $\mu \mathrm{m}$ particles, build-up of Cu in $>150 \mu \mathrm{~m}$ particles is highly influenced by different land use fractions rather than the traffic volume (Table 2). This implies that the influence exerted by sources specific to land use characteristics could overtake the influence of traffic volume in relation to the build-up of Cu associated with coarse particles.

The influence of particulate properties on the build-up of metals associated with $>150 \mu \mathrm{~m}$ particles shows significant differences compared to metals associated with $<150 \mu \mathrm{~m}$ particles. Among all metal oxides compared, Mn exhibits the highest positive influence on the build-up of most of the metals (Table 2). This is also evident from the cross validation results given in Appendix A, where metals such as $\mathrm{Cd}, \mathrm{Ni}$ and Zn show statistically significant relationships with $\mathrm{Mn}(p<0.01)$. This is attributed to the presence of favourable adsorption sites created by Mn oxide, which would interact with metal ions. The contribution of Mn oxide to the build-up of $\mathrm{Cd}, \mathrm{Ni}$ and Zn was found to be $28 \%, 32 \%$ and $25 \%$, respectively, which are higher than the influence exerted by other factors.

Furthermore, based on the regression coefficients given in Table 2, clay forming minerals such as albite, microcline and chlorite were found to influence the adsorption of Cr and Ni . It has been reported that there is a high affinity of Cr and Ni for inter-layer crystalline structure of silicates and for favourable adsorption sites, such as hydroxyl groups present on clay minerals, but their affinity for exchangeable reactive sites are low (Bradl, 2004; Nannoni and Protano, 2016). As evident from Table 2, Cr and Ni show inverse relationships with ECEC, while $\mathrm{Zn}, \mathrm{Cu}, \mathrm{Pb}$ and Cd have positive relationships with ECEC. This further implies that compared to Cr and Ni , the fraction of $\mathrm{Zn}, \mathrm{Cu}, \mathrm{Pb}$ and Cd in $>150 \mu \mathrm{~m}$ particles have a high

potential for desorption and for being mobilised. Among these metals, similar to $<150 \mu \mathrm{~m}$ particles, Cd in $>150 \mu \mathrm{~m}$ particles shows the highest positive relationship with ECEC.

As evident from Table 2, the regression coefficients in relation to antecedent dry days generally show inverse relationships with metals in $>150 \mu \mathrm{~m}$ fraction, except for Zn and Cu . This could be due to the unavailability of favourable charge sites to interact with metals. On the other hand, compared with fine particles, coarser particles have lower cation exchange capacities and specific surface areas that can limit metal adsorption capacity (Bradl, 2004; Djukić et al., 2016).

# 2.4 Practical implications of research outcomes 

The implementation of appropriate stormwater pollution mitigation strategies to eliminate potentially harmful effects of pollutants entrained in stormwater runoff is important to enhance stormwater quality and to safeguard urban water environments. Pollutant mitigation strategies need to be designed by understanding the characteristics of stormwater pollutants and associated pollutant processes, including different influential factors. The current study quantitatively assessed the degree of influence exerted by different factors on metal build-up using BN modelling and provides enhanced insights into the factors influencing build-up process variability.

The study revealed that the degree of influence exerted by external factors such as traffic and other land use specific characteristics on the build-up of metals varies from one metal ion to another. Therefore, the individual contribution of these factors should be incorporated into stormwater quality models when estimating the build-up loads of specific metal species. Furthermore, potential inconsistencies between factors influencing metal adsorption (i.e. particulate properties) were identified among different metal ions and particle size fractions. As such, it may not be sufficient to only account for the influence of external factors, but the mechanisms involved in metal adsorption to particles need to be understood. Additionally, the inherent factors influencing process variability (i.e. particle size, organic matter content and mineralogy) should also be incorporated into stormwater quality modelling in order to improve accuracy of the outcomes and thereby to develop more effective stormwater

pollution mitigation strategies. This in turn will facilitate the generation of knowledge on the uncertainty associated with stormwater quality modelling.

# 3 Conclusions 

The BN proposed in this study provides a framework to identify the relationships between factors driving variability associated with metal build-up on road surfaces and the relative ranking of influence exerted by these factors. The outcomes of the study revealed that $\mathrm{Zn}, \mathrm{Cu}$ and Cd associated with road dust particles have a positive relationship with daily traffic volume, implying that traffic volume would be a major source of those metals compared to the anthropogenic and natural sources specific to different land uses. However, the build-up of $\mathrm{Pb}, \mathrm{Cr}$ and Ni was found to be influenced by the fraction of a particular land use in a given area rather than traffic volume. Compared with the external factors, the variability in metal build-up was found to be highly influenced by the variability associated with the adsorption behaviour of metals on road surfaces. Depending on the particle size, the adsorption characteristics of metals for different soil constituents showed significant differences. This is attributed to the variability associated with pollutant composition in different land uses and differences in the chemical characteristics of adsorbents such as the availability of favourable adsorption sites. Therefore, it is important to account for the behavioural variability of individual metal species and the factors influencing their behaviour for deriving more accurate outcomes from stormwater quality modelling.
