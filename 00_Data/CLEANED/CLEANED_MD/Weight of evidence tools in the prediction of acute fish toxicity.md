# Weight of evidence tools in the prediction of acute fish toxicity 

Scott E. Belanger,Adam D. Lillicrap,S. Jannicke Moe,Raoul Wolf,Kristin
Connors,Michelle R. Embry
First published: 20 January 2022 https://doi.org/10.1002/ieam. 4581

## Abstract

Acute fish toxicity (AFT) is a key endpoint in nearly all regulatory implementations of environmental hazard assessments of chemicals globally. While it is an early tier assay, the AFT assay is complex and utilizes many juvenile fish each year for the registration and assessment of chemicals. Thus, it is imperative to seek animal alternative approaches to replace or reduce animal use for environmental hazard assessments. A Bayesian Network (BN) model has been developed that brings together a suite of lines of evidence (LoEs) to produce a probabilistic estimate of acute fish toxicity without the testing of additional juvenile fish. Lines of evidence include chemical descriptors, mode of action (MOA) assignment, knowledge of algal and daphnid acute toxicity, and animal alternative assays such as fish embryo tests and in vitro fish assays (e.g., gill cytotoxicity). The effort also includes retrieval, assessment and curation of quality acute fish toxicity data as these act as the baseline of comparison to model outputs. An ideal outcome of this effort would be to have global applicability, acceptance and uptake, relevance for predominant fish species used in chemical assessments, be expandable to allow incorporation of future knowledge and data be publicly available. The BN model can be conceived as having incorporated principles of tiered assessment and whose outcomes will be directed by the available evidence in combination with prior information. We demonstrate that as additional evidence is included into prediction of a given chemical's ecotoxicity profile, both the accuracy and the precision of the predicted AFT can increase. Ultimately an improved environmental hazard assessment will be achieved.

# Introduction 

Weight of Evidence (WoE) is frequently cited as being necessary for a wide variety of decisionmaking needs due to the complexity of environmental data (Hall et al. 2017). Understanding environmental fate, hazard and exposure for any well studied chemical will normally reveal the presence of conflicting data and the presence of novel test systems and endpoints. A WoE analysis of such diverse data requires judgment by technical experts with a range of expertise and philosophical leanings. While standardization of testing procedures can help somewhat in this regard, as the knowledge of individual chemicals' properties expands or uses of a chemical changes, it is appropriate to tailor the assessment of the chemical to those aspects which possess the greatest likelihood of informing decision-making and not be overly narrow in the application of standard test procedures. New endpoints or understanding subtle aspects of how a chemical interacts with biota will inject novel information into the decision-making process. Well known examples of this phenomenon are the use and application of genomic techniques in human and environmental assessments and the emergence of endocrine disruption as an environmental concern. New information may cause scientists to reassess what they thought they already knew or provide new avenues to consider when assessing a chemical entity.

Another relatively new and major area (for environmental assessment) is the use of animal alternative assays, often referred to as New Approach Methodologies or NAMs, to address endpoints of regulatory concern. Lillicrap et al. (2016) reviewed the general state-of the-science for various endpoints that require the use of fish for environmental assessment including bioaccumulation, acute and chronic toxicity and endocrine disruption. The acute fish toxicity (AFT) test (OECD 203, OECD 2019) is the most frequently used assay because it is a requirement in nearly all global regulatory schemes for the purposes of risk assessment and classification and labelling of chemicals (Burden et al. 2019). AFT tests account for the most fish used to satisfy regulatory testing requirements. While chronic (long-term) fish toxicity and bioaccumulation assays use more organisms per test, they are also used at a much lower frequency than AFT in the assessment of chemicals. Acute fish toxicity for effluent assessments in many countries utilize a large number of organisms and the employment of fish in research endeavors is a larger source of fish use as well (ECHA 2017). In our estimation, effluent testing

and research likely account for greater numbers than that where acute fish toxicity is a requirement for chemical registration (Norberg-King et al. 2018; ECHA 2017).

Bioethical concerns are a global driver for reducing the number of fish used in acute toxicity testing and replacing these tests with alternative in vivo, as well as in vitro and in silico approaches is a key objective. A wide range of regulatory, legal, and bioethical variability across the world makes the replacement of fish testing with NAMs extremely difficult. However, pressure on registrants of chemicals in commerce also continues to mount. In the absence of globally accepted recognition of replacement, reduction and refinement efforts, a reality is that acute fish toxicity tests may be required for registering a chemical in one region but not in another. Such dichotomies could be eliminated if evidence can be provided to convince scientists, and in particular regulatory authorities, that direct testing using juvenile fish (OECD 203) is unnecessary to decide if fish are the most sensitive taxonomic group for determining hazard . Lammer et al. (2009) and Belanger et al. (2013) provided substantial evidence that toxicity of chemicals to fish embryos was equivalent to typical fish acute toxicity. This assertion was challenged by Scholz et al. (2016), resulting in a subsequent opinion rendered by the ECHA (European Chemicals Agency) (2017) to industry and interested scientists to produce a WoE approach to fully support the use of fish embryo toxicity data (and other alternative assays/approaches) in place of AFT data in European chemical registrations.

# Review of on-going work regarding AFT 

Efforts to address the execution, utility, and interpretation of alternatives to acute fish toxicity have been on-going for almost 30 years. Early efforts by Nagel (1994) and colleagues led to a formalized fish embryo test for effluent testing which was standardized as an ISO Guideline in the mid 2000's and subsequently adopted as an OECD Test Guideline in 2013 (OECD 236; OECD 2013). The fish embryo test relies on certain apical endpoints to accurately predict lethality including coagulation (death), lack of heartbeat, lack of somite development, and nondetachment of the tail. Lammer et al. (2009) and Belanger et al. (2013) compared the fish embryo toxicity (FET) to the AFT and found nearly a 1:1 concordance for a large number of chemicals across a wide range of modes of action (Figure 1). This work formed the basis of the assertion by some European chemical registrants to waive the AFT in lieu of the FET, for which

ECHA since clarified could be done but in a broader WoE context. As a complicating matter, recent simulation studies of FET versus AFT study designs clearly show that the OECD recommended minimum fish per concentration $(\mathrm{n}=7)$ compromises certainty in the LC50 and confidence limit estimates relative to the study design recommended for fish embryos ( $\mathrm{n}=20$ per concentration with positive and negative controls) (Carr et al. 2018). Recent efforts to improve the approach to AFT in the OECD 203 Test Guideline process includes utilization of clinical signs that predict mortality (i.e., moribundity) and shortening the test duration (Katsiadaki et al. 2022).

Another alternative method is the implementation of the Threshold Approach (OECD 126, explained more fully below). The approach utilizes daphnid and algae toxicity tests to select the concentration at which to expose fish in a limit (threshold) test. Such an approach has been suggested as a means to reduce the use of fish by as much as $70 \%$ (Hutchinson et al. 2003). Although not explicitly expressed by Hutchinson et al. (2003), the Threshold Approach, can be considered a small WoE approach as it draws upon a variety of ecotoxicological insights to develop hazard conclusions.

Paparella et al. (2021) reviewed the known state of uncertainty and limitations of the acute fish toxicity test. The authors rightfully contend that the AFT has never been validated in the same sense that current OECD Test Guidelines require and as is outlined in OECD validation program documents under the Test Guidelines Programme (OECD 2005). The AFT, as currently practiced, has considerable drawbacks. Large variabilities in toxicity data for the same compound and species across laboratories are well documented in the literature (Belanger et al. 2013) and yet, AFT results are normally viewed as the gold standard against which alternative assays are judged. Regulatory inertia, tradition, and a lack of familiarity with strengths and weaknesses provided by alternative assays all seem to play a significant role in the lack of regulatory acceptance to move beyond in vivo testing (Lillicrap et al. 2016). Many advantages are afforded by alternative assays including increased statistical power, improved interlaboratory calibration and validation for new, more robust assays, higher throughput, and improved mechanistic insights to the chemical's activity (Carr et al. 2019; Lillicrap et al. 2016, Paparella et al. 2021). Combining these additional tools in a WoE approach has the potential to significantly improve the environmental hazard and risk assessment of chemicals rather than relying on only

whether an organism simply lives or dies, which at the population level is too crude to ensure the protection of the environment. While acute toxicity may be useful for generating information on potency of various chemicals, it has lower relevance for environmental hazard assessment where longer term, lower-level exposures and prediction of long-term effects and environmental behaviors are more important. Yet, the AFT assay remains a mandatory requirement in most regulatory schemes.

AFT is but one of several in vivo fish assays that are used to inform environmental decisions of chemicals. QSARs, chronic (long-term) fish toxicity, bioaccumulation, endocrine disruption and field surveys all find a home in the comprehensive assessment of potential environmental perturbations and chemical exposures to fish. In 2012, the OECD brought together a group of scientists to consider the integration of all assays utilizing fish, with the goal of more efficiently using those assays to inform each other and reduce testing burdens resulting in the Fish Framework document (OECD 212). Fish embryo testing was foreseen as a future input into the fish assay toolkit (and was also identified as such as early as 2008 in guidance documents for REACH; ECHA 2008). Figure 2 displays one possible outcome of an integrated view on fish testing. As with the discussions above, this is also a form of WoE which is mentioned prominently throughout the Fish Framework document and its recommendations for future action. The OECD workshop identified that the following strategies could be implemented to reduce in vivo testing: limit tests, Threshold approach, step-down approach, screening methodologies that do not utilize animals, (such as (Q)SAR tools, in vitro assays, or read-across), and Fish embryo tests (FET). The FET is considered a replacement or refinement assay depending on the regulatory jurisdiction owing to the utilization of the non-exogenous feeding, embryonic stage of development (Strahle et al. 2012).

Clearly an integrative process is needed to holistically address the types of information and data available to inform the acute toxicity of chemicals to fish. The sources of information are diverse, vary in quantitative rigor, and chemical coverage (when considering for example, different types of alternative assays). In acknowledgement of this, Moe et al. (2020) and Lillicrap et al. (2020) initiated an effort to construct a quantitative model to integrate all sources of information using a Bayesian network approach. Thus, three closely related assays (acute fish toxicity, fish gill cytotoxicity, and fish embryo toxicity) may be cross-referenced to inform acute fish toxicity of previously untested chemicals. Belanger et al. (2013), Tannenberger et al. (2013), Sobanska et al. (2018), and Fischer et al. (2019) each explored different aspects of the complexities involved through comparisons of outcomes amongst these assays. Belanger et al. (Figure 1) compared the toxicity of approximately 150 different compounds to FET and AFT (tests on the same compound) and found a near 1:1 concordance. However, variation in the effect data for the same chemical sometimes spanned orders of magnitude within either the AFT (more frequently) versus the FET. This is likely due to the heterogeneity particularly in the AFT data with respect to species choice (many species are recommended for use by OECD), test conditions, organism size, water source, and utilization of analytical (or not) confirmation. The comparisons were performed using orthogonal regression, a multivariate approach that allows for variation in both x and y dimensions (unlike standard linear regression where the independent x -variable has fixed values and the dependent $y$-variable is predicted with variation). Tannenberger et al. (2013) developed a different database where the AFT focused solely on fathead minnow, which constrains inter-species variability but simultaneously constrains AFT comparisons using species fully recommended and endorsed by OECD (OECD 2019). Figure 3A shows comparisons for cytotoxicity and in vivo toxicity for 37 compounds. Some, but not all, toxicity data in this Figure are also presented in that of Figure 1. Fischer et al. (2019), as part of the international round robin trial for the gill cytotoxicity assay, also summarized the comparative in vitro gill toxicity to the AFT (Fig. 3B). Again, as the gill cytotoxicity assay is relatively new, the breadth of coverage is less (albeit rapidly growing, cf. Natsch et al. 2018) and will partially intersect with that of the FET and AFT. Importantly, the FET and gill cytotoxicity assay have both undergone international validation and extensive ring trials to quantify their intra- and inter-laboratory variabilities as well as a demonstration of transferability to naïve laboratories and is now presently identified as OECD Test Guideline 249, "Fish Cell Line Acute Toxicity: The RTgillW1 cell line assay". Sobanska and colleagues presented a different view of the FET-AFT relationship that highlights some uncertainties in the newer assay (see Fig 4 and Sobanska et al. 2018). Quantification of chemical exposures identified a number of uncertainties which were subsequently resolved (Birke and Scholz 2019), but others remain, especially those with specific modes of action (see discussion

also below). As debated at the FET Workshop hosted by ECHA in 2017 (ECHA 2017), a mechanism is urgently needed to weigh results, predictions and ultimately derive predicted AFT when an in vivo test is not available to meet increasing expectations by society, regulators and scientists with regards to improved animal welfare.

An unrelated, but similarly complex module of a potential WoE scheme is that of the threshold approach (TA, OECD 126). The Threshold Approach, by itself, has also been posited as a WoE approach. In the Threshold Approach, acute toxicity tests are first performed on algae (unspecified species, but likely one of the small number of internationally accepted test species in OECD 201, OECD 2011) and Daphnia sp. (OECD 202, 2004). The more sensitive species of algae and Daphnia is identified by the lower of the two effect values (the concentration causing a $50 \%$ effect - EC50). This concentration is then used as the test concentration in an acute toxicity limit test (i.e., 1 concentration at the threshold value of toxicity). If fish are not affected at this threshold concentration (i.e., $0 \%$ mortality), the assay confirms that fish are less sensitive and the assessor can proceed with confidence that the lower hazard value from the algae and daphnid tests will adequately inform the risk assessment. If fish are affected at the threshold concentration, then a full acute fish toxicity test is required. An easy extension to this thinking is to replace the AFT with that of the FET instead, thereby excluding the need for juvenile fish altogether. Rawlings et al. (2019) explored various permutations of algae-daphnid-AFT-FET testing to determine if the use of the FET or AFT impacted either the most sensitive hazard value or GHS classification. The authors conclude that there is no distinction (advantage) of using the AFT instead of the FET in the threshold approach and they can be considered equivalent. Such comparisons can also be used to assist testing prioritization and weightings applied to each assay for assessing overall acute fish toxicity. Rawlings and Belanger (personal communication, unpublished data) extended the threshold approach concepts to QSARs and found that while quantitative differences existed when using QSARs versus "real" data, the trends were remarkably similar albeit slightly higher uncertainty.

Scientists researching alternative methods consistently agree that identification of the mode of action (MOA) is an essential aspect of understanding toxicity relationships among chemicals and assays (Kienzler et al. 2017). Schirmer et al. (2006), Belanger et al. (2013), Tannenberger et al. (2013), Sobanska et al. (2018) and Fisher et al. (2019) use MOA assignments in parsing out

trends within the various databases used for comparing fish acute toxicity across compounds. This is particularly important as it is becoming well established that neurotoxicants represent a unique class of chemicals based on their MOA that are less well predicted by alternative assays (suggesting somewhat more frequently the FET and cytotoxicity tests, for example, are less sensitive to this particular mode of action) compared to the AFT. This is in conflict with the observation that neurotoxicants are also somewhat less toxic to fish overall than they are to invertebrates (Threshold Approach thinking, Connors et al. 2019). MOA is therefore critical in the development of WoE for predicting AFT. Kienzler et al. (2017) developed a MOA comparison for hundreds of compounds using several assignment schemes. These, like other databases, are built from different data sets, include different compounds, and were truly developed for different purposes or emphases. Subsequently, Kienzler et al. (2019) established a consensus approach to unify the MOA schemes with a single outcome based on physical-chemical information and multifaceted expert opinions, again forming a type of WoE.

Other factors may contribute to informing fish toxicity including collation of highly diverse fish metabolism and physiology information that can be useful to support MOA and understanding the potential for metabolic activation of unusual or specific compounds required to invoke toxicity to fish. While limited cases for such activation exist (allyl alcohol for example, see Kluver et al. 2014), these can explain outliers or trend busters for FET-AFT or cytotoxicity-AFT relationships (UBA 2020). Evolving neural toxicity assays, such as the quantification of the "touch-evoke" response or other behavioral endpoints using video tracking software, can more definitively determine the likelihood that a toxicant is or is not a neurotoxicant and thus lead to reliance on certain LoEs (e.g., the FET) versus others in predicting the AFT. The assignment of MOA will remain a significant area of research and will likely expand to include additional behavioral assessments such as swimming speed, endurance, and hyperactivity/hypoactivity.

## The Acute Fish Toxicity Bayesian Network Model as a Path to Resolution

As indicated previously, Moe et al. (2020) and Lillicrap et al. (2020) provided the framework for developing a quantitative predictor of AFT using a Bayesian network (BN) model including multiple existing and proposed LoEs. BNs are graphical, probabilistic and potentially causal models, and have been used increasingly in hazard and risk assessment during the last decades


baseline from which valid and accepted QSAR models can be derived and used for the BN. FET and fish gill cytotoxicity are considered strong, direct evidence in support of AFT based on earlier reviews of these assays. The Threshold Approach (TA), as described earlier, is considered supportive and indirect evidence of AFT. The outcomes of the TA is frequently more conservative (lower LC/EC50) than for the full acute fish toxicity test. When used in combination with other LoEs the TA can further support AFT conclusions. MOA assignment is particularly challenging as several accepted classification systems exist. For the further development of BN, the integrated consensus approach given in EnviroTox (http://www.envirotoxdatabase.org/) is utilized (see Kienzler et al 2019). MOA can be useful to identify compounds that are likely more toxic to fish (e.g., selected neurotoxicants) or other organisms (e.g., herbicides being more toxic to algae). This can feed into the threshold approach for supporting indirect LoEs and allows investigators to "make sense" of either variably toxic compounds or the likelihood that a single individual assay is correct. Newer LoEs such as gathering knowledge of biochemical and metabolic pathways or behavioral/physiological assays can be used to inform the potential of metabolism (often reducing internal toxic burdens for a compound), metabolic activation (making a compound more toxic potentially), and whether these fit into existing MOA frameworks.

Here, we have explored more systematically the importance of the number of LoEs in data-rich vs. data-poor scenarios using two example substances (Figure 6): Triclosan (high toxicity to fish) and tetradecyl sulphate (medium toxicity). For each LoE, the BN model uses either evidence (data) for the given substance or a prior probability distribution based on all training data, depending on the scenario. In the example with triclosan, all scenarios with evidence for four or three LOEs give the correct model prediction as indicated by high quality AFT results. When evidence for only two LoEs are used, the toxicity level is underestimated in 2 out of 6 cases. When evidence for only one LoE is used, toxicity is even more strongly underestimated in 2 out of 4 cases. The BN predicts the correct interval for all scenarios where embryo data are used. In the case of tetradecyl sulfate, the prediction accuracy (correct interval have highest probability) increases with the number of LoEs with evidence. Compared to triclosan, the predictions for tetradecyl sulfate tend to have lower precision (the peak probabilities don't get as high) and a bit lower accuracy (only 8 out of 15 have correct interval, compared to 11 out of 15 for triclosan).

To generalize from these two examples, inclusion of evidence from a larger number of LoEs in this BN model seems to improve both accuracy (more cases of correctly predicted toxicity interval) and precision (higher probability of the predicted most probable interval). We also expect that inclusion of more replicate values (repetitive tests on the same endpoints) within an LoE would give more precise AFT predictions (that is, narrower probability distributions). This aspect will be further analyzed in the current SWiFT project. Actual AFT data is used within the model evaluation. Of course, this assumes that the AFT data is precise and accurate, therefore, an effort to assess reliability of toxicity tests and curate detailed results is also part of the effort. It is important to recognize that the BN model utilizes all data to understand variance in measured endpoints. This is an improvement over the common practice of using geometric mean values to summarize multiple measurements for the same taxon (Stephan et al. 1985; ECHA 2008).

Figure 5 displays how LoEs are combined in the BN model. Within each LoE (pathway), toxicity to fish is predicted from one or more nodes. The toxicity nodes are arbitrarily discretized into 5 toxicity levels, based on intervals of effect concentration (LC50): very low ( $>100 \mathrm{mg} / \mathrm{L}$ ), low (5$100 \mathrm{mg} / \mathrm{L}$ ), medium $(0.5-5 \mathrm{mg} / \mathrm{L})$, high $(0.5-0.01 \mathrm{mg} / \mathrm{L})$, and very high $(<0.01 \mathrm{mg} / \mathrm{L})$. These toxicity levels are relevant since aquatic toxicity not only used as environmental risk assessment inputs for PNEC (Predicted No Effect Concentration for ecosystems) determination, but also for classification and labelling under the Globally Harmonized System. For all parent nodes (with no incoming arrows), prior probability distributions were calculated based on all substances in the dataset. Each child node has a conditional probability table (CPT) that is used for calculating the posterior probability distribution dependent on the probability distribution of the parent node, when the model is run for a given chemical. The CPTs are constructed both from expert judgement (e.g., assignment of chemical categories) and from empirical relationships (e.g., frequency distributions of toxicity levels in different chemical categories).

Table 1 (inputs for the BN) can be thought of in a tiered approach quite easily. Direct LoE provide the best evidence for AFT, assuming a high-quality assay is performed. The more LoE

employed, the higher the tier. Outputs are provided along the way as a probability for a particular outcome. This is a similar process to the historical tiering of aquatic toxicity data given in Cairns and Dickson (1978) and described in Menzie et al. (2021). The more LoEs that are implemented and the more evidence within each LoE that is gathered, both the accuracy and precision of the prediction should ideally improve. As with conventional risk assessment paradigms, the level of uncertainty that can be tolerated in a given environmental decision is related to the magnitude of the difference between hazard and exposure concentrations and the precision required for acceptance.

The AFT BN model is a quantitative approach to WoE (while many others are qualitative, see Suter et al. 2017). The BN can conceivably be mapped to a WoE framework. The weighting in a traditional WoE could be implemented as probability distributions in a BN, both within individual LoEs and for integrating the LoEs (Moe et al. 2021). The assignment of conditional probabilities to different variables within in a LoE in the BN corresponds to setting weights to pieces of evidence in a WoE (Moe et al. 2021). Assigning a lower weight to a piece of evidence can be obtained by setting wide probability distributions (representing high uncertainty or variability) in the relationship from this node to its child node. Within a line of evidence the calculation of posterior probabilities for the last child node of the line then accumulates the weights given to all parent nodes in this line. It follows that when using the BN to predict the toxicity to juvenile fish from all LoE for a chemical of concern, the weighting of the total evidence for each hypothesis in a WoE (Suter et al., 2017a) can correspond to calculating the posterior probability of each toxicity level ("very low", "low" etc.) in the BN.

# What a successful WoE for AFT Looks Like 

Regulatory acceptance of animal alternative approaches is challenging. There are many reasons for this including the structure of regulations which vary across the globe, the dependence on vertebrate data in historical assessments which could be "re-visited" if reliance on alternatives take hold, the uneven levels of expertise in the scientific and regulatory communities, and fastpaced evolving regulatory changes which "move, and often raise, the bar". Due to these and many other factors, a successful WoE for AFT would possess the following attributes:

1. The approach would have global applicability;

2. Outcomes would be met with regulatory acceptance and not require significant changes to legislations or federal law;
3. Would be relevant equally for all fish species whose use varies across regulatory jurisdictions (e.g., Europe employs zebrafish most frequently, the US uses fathead minnow, Canada and the UK rely heavily upon rainbow trout, Japan uses Medaka preferentially and movement exists for China to rely upon the Chinese rare minnow).
4. The model leaves open for expansion of knowledge regarding chemical domain, new LoEs, and underlying input data;
5. Would include a renewed recognition that the "gold standard" AFT assay, is not necessarily more precise and accurate, thus, improving the transparency and curation of accepted highest quality AFT data is critical;
6. Would improve the environmental hazard and risk assessment of chemicals;
7. Be publicly available, but controlled, to ensure transparent application.

Beyond AFT, other areas of fish hazard assessment could benefit from a similar approach (and some are already in progress). Bioaccumulation assessment is identified in ECHA $(2008,2017)$ as being a WoE process and is discussed in more detail within this special issue by Arnot and colleagues (Arnot et al. 2021, in this Special Series) and previously by Lillicrap et al. (2016). Endocrine disruption and long-term fish toxicity were identified in OECD (2012) as also requiring WoE development. Bioaccumulation, endocrine disruption (also discussed by Mihaich and Burgoon et al. (2021) (in this Special Series) and long-term fish toxicity all involve assessment of complex endpoints that require deep expertise. A transparent, accepted WoE process for all would benefit the regulatory environment in addition to the early development of new chemical entities. Furthermore, animal alternative assays will play a pivotal role in future WoE approaches as already identified by Volz et al. 2011 and Villeneuve et al. 2014 for longterm fish toxicity, Nichols et al. (2018) for bioaccumulation and Scholz et al. (2014) for potential endocrine disruption. In all of these, the guiding principles for WoE will be the same but a much wider net of lines of evidence will likely be needed. Whether Bayesian approaches would fulill some of these needs is yet to be answered.

Many remaining challenges exist, including how to deal with novel chemicals which may not have much context for their use, MOA and properties. That said, the surface has been scratched

with respect to existing compounds whose assessments are part of the historical record, but lack animal alternative data. Compounds with difficult solubility, sorptivity, analytical verification and toxic properties will also remain challenging. Importantly, regulators and scientists will need to possess courage to make decisions when data are less than perfect (which will always be the situation) and develop new regulatory inertia in the face of a lack of precedents. Finally, since environmental risk assessment frameworks and chemical legislations are principally employed for the protection of the environment, hazard assessment strategies should be moving away from relying on acute fish toxicity for classification purposes and focus more on mechanistic/sublethal endpoints to identify chemicals of concern.
